# ENG 362 — English Drama (19th & 20th Century) (Exam Study Guide)

## Unit overview

This Unit surveys English and European drama from the early nineteenth century’s melodramatic spectacle to the post‑modern experiments of Caryl Churchill and beyond. The period begins with **broad‑way melodrama**, whose clear moral oppositions (virtue rewarded, vice punished) and musical accompaniment (the “call‑boy” scene) catered to a popular taste for sensational revelation. Into this space stepped **Henrik Ibsen**, whose *Hedda Gabler* and *A Doll’s House* pioneered **realist drama** on the domestic interior: family secrets become social critique. Ibsen’s influence runs through **George Bernard Shaw**, who weaponised wit to advance socialism and women’s education (*Man and Superman*, *Pygmalion*), and **Oscar Wilde**, whose *The Importance of Being Earnest* is a flawless “comedy of manners” that mocks Victorian hypocrisy while delighting in epigram. **Richard Brinsley Sheridan** (*The School for Scandal*) pre‑figures the manners comedy in its satirical portrait of London society.

The second half of the Unit maps the crisis of meaning that followed: **modernist drama** moves from T.S. Eliot’s highly ritualised verse drama (*Murder in the Cathedral*) through the **Theatre of the Absurd** (Samuel Beckett’s *Waiting for Godot*, Eugène Ionesco, Tom Stoppard), which portrays a universe without clear meaning — characters wait, repeat, and speak past one another. **Harold Pinter’s** “comedies of menace” use subtext, silence and the famous “Pinter pause” to expose power games beneath social exchanges. The Unit concludes with **feminist and post‑colonial political theatre**: **Caryl Churchill** shatters chronology and gender norms (*Top Girls*, *Cloud Nine*, *Far Away*), while contemporary dramatists such as **August Wilson**, **Suzan‑Lori Parks** and **Jackie Sibblies Drury** engage race, migration and environmental crisis through devised and physical theatre techniques.

## Learning outcomes

- Identify and differentiate the major movements of 19th/20th‑century drama: melodrama, realism, absurd, political and post‑modern.
- Analyse how dramatic conventions (aside, soliloquy, subtext, silence) shape meaning and audience response.
- Compare the treatment of social issues across the realist, absurdist and political traditions.
- Evaluate form‑content relationships in modern drama.
- Situate the works of key dramatists within their historical moments.
- Assess the continuing relevance of these plays for contemporary Kenyan performance.

## Detailed notes

### From melodrama to realism: Sheridan, Ibsen, Shaw, Wilde

**Melodrama** (the dominant popular form c. 1800–50) offered clear moral dualisms resolved by the hero’s triumph, underscored by live music. **Richard Brinsley Sheridan** (*The School for Scandal*, 1777) and **Richard Sheridan** moved the genre toward wit — the “comedy of manners” satirising urban society’s gossip and hypocrisy. **Realist drama** arrived with **Henrik Ibsen**: *A Doll’s House* (1879) stages the “torpedo‑room” of a marriage, exposing how social convention destroys individual fulfilment; *Hedda Gabler* locates neurosis in the drawing‑room. Realist conventions include the single set, continuous time, and **subtext** — what characters do not say aloud.

**George Bernard Shaw** transformed British theatre into a forum for social argument. *Man and Superman* stages the battle between romanticidealism and Darwinian realism; *Pygmalion* (adapted as *My Fair Lady*) critiques class mobility and linguistic snobbery. Shaw’s **“heart‑ballast”** theory holds that ideas, not emotion, move the audience. **Oscar Wilde’s *The Importance of Being Earnest*** (1895) is the summit of the comedy of manners: every line is an epigram, and the trivial “lost handbag” plot gently mocks society’s trivial concerns while celebrating the performative nature of identity (“I never travel without my diary”).

### Modernist and absurdist theatre: Eliot, Beckett, Ionesco, Stoppard

Modernism turned drama inward and upward — toward ritual, myth and linguistic experiment. **T.S. Eliot’s** verse dramas (*Murder in the Cathedral*, *The Family Reunion*) reject naturalistic dialogue in favour of **choral commentary** and **ritualised action**. The **Theatre of the Absurd** — **Samuel Beckett** (*Waiting for Godot*, *End‑Godot*), **Eugène Ionesco** (*The Bald Soprano*), **Tom Stoppard** (*Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead*) — portrays a world without God, without progress, without communication. Beckett’s Vladimir and Estragon wait for someone who never comes; their circular conversation and comic repetition suggest that meaning itself may be **performative** rather than inherent. Stoppard’s *Rosencrantz* reframes Shakespeare’s *Hamlet* from minor characters’ perspectives, asking who gets a story and who remains background.

### Pinter, Churchill and political/feminist drama

**Harold Pinter** (1930–2008) called his plays “comedies of menace”: beneath witty, mundane conversation lies threat and the exercise of power. **Pinteresque dialogue** — non‑sequiturs, interruptions, the pregnant pause — reveals the gap between what people mean and what they say. **Caryl Churchill** broke formal bounds to advance feminist politics. *Top Girls* stages a mythical dinner party of women across centuries; *Cloud Nine* uses **cross‑casting** across gender, race and time to expose colonial and patriarchal power; *Far Away* presents a world where nature itself is at war. Contemporary Churchill‑inspired work — by Kenyan playwrights such as **Wahumba Guptil** or **Sifa Gicheru** — continues to use **devised theatre** to address land, gender and post‑colonial identity.

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

### Short answer
1. **(Easy)** Define “comedy of manners.”
2. **(Medium)** What is a “Pinter pause” and what does it do?
3. **(Medium)** Explain the relationship between absurdist drama and existentialism.
4. **(Hard)** How does Churchill use cross‑casting in *Cloud Nine*?

### Essay questions
1. **(Medium)** “Drama is a mirror of society.” How far do Shaw, Wilde and Churchill share this view?
2. **(Hard)** Analyse the role of silence and subtext in Pinter’s drama.
3. **(Hard)** Compare the treatment of identity in *The Importance of Being Earnest* and *Cloud Nine*.

### Objective / passage-based
1. **(Easy)** *Waiting for Godot* is a prime example of:
   - A) Kitchen‑sink drama B) Theatre of the Absurd C) Comedy of manners D) Epic theatre
2. **(Medium)** “I never travel without my diary.” Whose line is this and what does it reveal about identity?
3. **(Hard)** Extract: “Nothing is more futile than a single, isolated, individual action.” Name the thinker and the character who echoes this in drama.
