## Unit overview

Study Skills, Research Methods and ICT provides the practical toolkit that underpins all degree-level study in the B.Ed (Arts). The unit covers the formulation of research questions, the evaluation of sources, systematic note-taking, bibliography construction and the use of information and communication technologies for academic work. For Kenyan students entering university from diverse secondary-school backgrounds, the unit addresses a critical skills gap: how to move beyond classroom note-taking to independent, library-based research. It also prepares students for the research demands of later units, including literature essays, action-research projects and the final-year thesis.

The unit is particularly relevant in a Kenyan context where digital access to academic materials is uneven. Students learn to navigate the University of Nairobi library catalogue, access databases such as JSTOR and Project MUSE, and use reference managers such as Zotero. They also develop habits of work — file organisation, regular backup, draft revision — that support academic integrity and reduce the pressure of deadlines. These transferable skills are highly valued by employers in education, publishing, civil society and the public sector.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this unit, students should be able to:
- Distinguish between primary and secondary sources and evaluate their reliability.
- Construct focused, manageable and arguable research questions.
- Locate relevant academic materials using library catalogues, databases and search strategies.
- Take reasoned, systematic notes and construct a bibliography using standard conventions.
- Use ICT tools — word processors, reference managers, file organisers — effectively for research.
- Reflect critically on the ethical dimensions of research, including plagiarism and source evaluation.

## Detailed notes

### Research questions and secondary criticism

Good literary research begins with a question that is specific, manageable and arguable. "How does Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o use symbolism in *A Grain of Wheat*?" is preferable to "Write about Ngũgĩ's novels." Once a question is framed, **secondary criticism** — journal articles, monographs, edited collections — helps map what has been written and where gaps remain. Students should use the library catalogue, JSTOR, Project MUSE and the university's subscribed databases. Keeping a **research log** of searches, sources and reflections prevents duplication and builds intellectual ownership of the topic. A typical research log records the database or catalogue used, the search terms, the number of results, the sources selected and the date accessed.

### Evaluating sources

Not all sources are equal. Ask: Who wrote this and what are their credentials? When was it published? Who published it — a scholarly press, a trade publisher or an unvetted website? Is the argument supported by evidence? Are other scholars cited and engaged critically? **Peer-reviewed articles** and **university-press monographs** are most reliable; websites require careful corroboration. In the Kenyan context, theses from Kenyan universities, working papers from research institutes and government documents (KNEC syllabi, KICD reports) are valuable primary and secondary sources. The CRAAP test (Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose) offers a practical checklist.

### Note-taking and bibliography building

Effective note-taking distinguishes between direct quotation, paraphrase and personal comment. A common system is to use a digital document with three columns: the page or URL, the exact words (if quoted), and a summary in your own words. Always record full publication details at the point of note-taking; reconstructing a bibliography from memory leads to errors. Bibliographies should follow the Harvard (author-date) system consistently. University guidelines for theses often require a separate annotated bibliography, summarising each source's argument and assessing its relevance.

### ICT tools for research

Practical ICT skills include using reference managers (Zotero, Mendeley) to store citations and generate reference lists; searching databases with **Boolean operators** (AND, OR, NOT); and maintaining a logical folder structure for drafts, sources and final essays. Students should also learn to back up work regularly, share drafts safely and verify the credibility of online sources. In Kenyan universities, where internet connectivity can be intermittent, saving work locally and printing drafts in advance are essential habits.

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

**Short answer**
1. Distinguish between a primary and a secondary source, giving one example relevant to the study of *Weep Not, Child*. (Easy)
2. Explain why peer review is an important marker of scholarly reliability. (Easy)
3. What is an annotated bibliography and what purpose does it serve in research? (Medium)

**Essay questions**
1. "A research question should be narrow enough to be manageable but broad enough to be significant." Discuss with reference to the process of literary research. (Hard)
2. Evaluate the strengths and limitations of digital databases such as JSTOR for research on African literature. (Medium)
3. How does systematic note-taking contribute to original argumentation in an essay? (Medium)

**Objective / passage-based**
1. Evaluate the following four sources for a research essay on oral literature in Kenya. For each, state whether it is primary or secondary, assess its likely reliability and suggest one question you would ask about it. (Hard)
   a. A student blog post discussing Gĩkũyũ folktales. (3 marks)
   b. A peer-reviewed article in *English in Africa* on East African oral narrative. (3 marks)
   c. The 2017 KICD English curriculum document. (3 marks)
   d. A Wikipedia entry on Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o. (3 marks)
