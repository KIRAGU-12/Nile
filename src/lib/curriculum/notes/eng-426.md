## Unit overview

Twentieth-Century English Literature traces the transformation of literary form and theme from the early modernist experiments of the early 1900s through the post-war, postcolonial, and contemporary periods. The unit examines how writers responded to the trauma of two world wars, the collapse of empire, and the rise of mass culture by breaking with Victorian realism and inventing new modes of representation. Students encounter the major techniques — fragmentation, stream of consciousness, montage, magical realism, and metafiction — that define the century's literary landscape.

The unit also positions English-language writing within a global frame, tracing how postcolonial authors brought non-European experiences into the centre of the English literary tradition. Writers such as Salman Rushdie and Kazuo Ishiguro use the resources of the novel to explore identity, history, and displacement, while the emergence of the 'global novel' raises questions about the politics of English as a literary language. Comparative Literature angles surface throughout, inviting students to consider how twentieth-century writing in English relates to modernist movements in other literatures and how translation and migration shape literary production.

By the end of the unit, students should be able to read modernist and postmodernist texts with confidence, identify the formal strategies authors deploy, and situate literary works within their historical and cultural contexts. The unit builds directly on the close-reading skills developed in earlier years and prepares students for the independent research required in the dissertation.

## Learning outcomes

- Analyse modernist techniques of fragmentation, stream of consciousness, and montage in prose and poetry.
- Interpret how war, empire, and mass culture reshape literary form and theme across the century.
- Evaluate the shift from modernism to postmodernism and the emergence of world literatures in English.
- Apply concepts from Comparative Literature (e.g. influence, translation, world-systems) to twentieth-century texts.
- Distinguish the characteristics of magical realism and postcolonial writing in English as distinct but overlapping modes.
- Construct well-supported arguments about the relationship between historical context and literary innovation.

## Detailed notes

### Modernism and its origins

Modernism emerged in the early twentieth century as a radical break with Victorian realism and moralising narrative. Influenced by Freudian psychology, Einstein's relativity, and the trauma of the First World War, modernist writers sought new forms that could represent a fragmented, uncertain world. Key techniques include **fragmentation** (non-linear narrative structure), **collage** (juxtaposing disparate voices and sources), and **intertextual allusion** (referencing other texts to layer meaning). The movement is associated with figures such as T.S. Eliot, Virginia Woolf, James Joyce, and Ezra Pound.

### Stream of consciousness

**Stream of consciousness** is a narrative mode that attempts to reproduce the flow of a character's inner thoughts without the mediation of an omniscient narrator. Virginia Woolf's *Mrs Dalloway* (1925) is a landmark example: the novel follows Clarissa Dalloway's preparations for a party over a single day, moving fluidly between her perceptions, memories, and the thoughts of those around her. The technique collapses past and present, interior and exterior, creating a psychological depth that traditional narration could not achieve. Joyce's *Ulysses* (1922) pushes the method further, using interior monologue to map an ordinary Dublin day onto the structure of Homer's *Odyssey*.

### Post-war literature and totalitarianism

After 1945, English-language literature grappled with the horrors of totalitarianism and genocide. George Orwell's *Nineteen Eighty-Four* (1949) and *Animal Farm* (1945) are direct political interventions that expose the mechanisms of authoritarian control — surveillance, propaganda, and the rewriting of history. The post-war period also saw the rise of the Holocaust novel and the literature of disillusionment, as writers questioned whether art could make sense of industrialised atrocity.

### Postmodernism

**Postmodernism** in literature is characterised by **parody**, **pastiche**, **metafiction** (fiction that self-consciously addresses its own status as fiction), and the blurring of high and low culture. Writers such as Thomas Pynchon and John Barth embrace these strategies to challenge the idea that literature can offer stable, unified meaning. Postmodern fiction often features unreliable narrators, fragmented timelines, and playful intertextuality, reflecting a worldview in which grand narratives (progress, enlightenment, empire) have been discredited.

### Magical realism and postcolonial writing in English

**Magical realism** blends realistic narrative with fantastical elements presented as ordinary. Salman Rushdie's *Midnight's Children* (1981) is the defining example: the protagonist Saleem Sinai is born at the exact moment of Indian independence, and his telepathic powers mirror the nation's turbulent history. Rushdie uses magical realism to explore how personal and national histories intertwine, making the extraordinary feel inevitable. This mode has been taken up by writers across the postcolonial world, from Gabriel García Márquez to Ben Okri.

### Postcolonial writing and the global novel

The 'global novel' describes fiction that crosses national and linguistic boundaries, bringing postcolonial perspectives into the mainstream of English literature. Writers such as Rushdie, Kazuo Ishiguro, and Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie write from positions of cultural translation, using English — the language of the former empire — to tell stories that challenge Western perspectives. Comparative Literature angles include the study of influence (how postcolonial writers respond to canonical English texts), the politics of translation, and the concept of **world literature** as a field that decentres the Euro-American canon.

### Comparative Literature dimensions

Comparative Literature in this context involves reading English-language texts alongside works from other traditions, examining how modernism and postmodernism manifest differently across cultures. Students should consider how Rushdie's magical realism relates to Latin American magical realism, how Ishiguro's unreliable narration echoes Japanese literary aesthetics, and how the English novel has been transformed by the inclusion of formerly colonised voices.

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

### Short answer

1. Define stream of consciousness and identify two techniques Woolf uses to achieve it in *Mrs Dalloway*.
2. What is the difference between modernism and postmodernism in literary form?
3. Explain the concept of magical realism with reference to *Midnight's Children*.
4. What does 'the global novel' mean in the context of twentieth-century English literature?
5. How does Orwell use satire in *Animal Farm* to critique totalitarianism?

### Essay questions

6. 'Modernist writers broke with tradition not for its own sake but to represent a shattered world.' Discuss with reference to at least two texts.
7. Compare the treatment of national history in *Midnight's Children* with one other postcolonial novel studied on the unit.
8. How does postmodern fiction challenge the reader's expectations? Use at least two examples.

### Objective / passage-based

9. (Difficulty: Easy) Identify the narrative mode used in the following passage from *Mrs Dalloway*: 'She felt somehow very like him — the young man who had killed himself.' (Options: direct discourse, reported thought, free indirect discourse, interior monologue)
10. (Difficulty: Medium) Read the opening of *The Waste Land* and identify two modernist features (e.g. fragmentation, allusion, juxtaposition of voices).
11. (Difficulty: Hard) Passage-based: Given an extract from a postcolonial novel that blends realistic and fantastical elements, explain how the author uses magical realism to comment on national identity.
