## Unit overview

Research Project / Dissertation is the capstone unit of the B.Ed (Arts) in English and Literature programme. It requires students to undertake an independent research project under supervision, applying the methods of literary studies and/or applied linguistics to a topic in English and Literature. The unit develops students' ability to formulate a research question, design a feasible methodology, locate the project in relevant theoretical and critical literature, and present findings clearly in writing following academic conventions.

The unit covers a range of research methods — library-based archival research, empirical methods (surveys, interviews, classroom observation), and creative-critical approaches (a critical commentary plus a creative piece) — and addresses the practical and ethical dimensions of research: ethics approval, proposal writing, citation conventions (APA, MLA), timeline management, and supervisor relationships. The unit also surfaces **Creative Writing** as a legitimate research method (the creative-critical dissertation) and **English for Specific Purposes (ESP)** as a potential research focus (e.g. investigating ESP practices in Kenyan secondary schools).

By the end of the unit, students should be able to formulate a research question and design a feasible methodology, locate the project in relevant theoretical and critical literature, and present findings clearly in writing following academic conventions.

## Learning outcomes

- Formulate a research question and design a feasible methodology.
- Locate the project in relevant theoretical and critical literature.
- Present findings clearly in writing following academic conventions.
- Apply appropriate citation and referencing styles (APA, MLA).
- Manage a research timeline and maintain effective communication with a supervisor.
- Identify and address ethical considerations in literary and linguistic research.

## Detailed notes

### Research methods

Research projects in English and Literature may take several forms. **Library-based research** involves archival work, critical editions, textual scholarship, corpus analysis, and theoretical readings. It requires skills in locating and evaluating primary and secondary sources, often using library catalogues, databases (JSTOR, Project MUSE), and citation indexes. **Empirical research** involves the collection of original data through surveys, interviews, focus groups, or classroom observation. Empirical projects require ethics approval from the university's ethics committee, informed consent from participants, and careful attention to data protection and confidentiality. **Creative-critical research** combines a creative piece (a poem, short story, play, or novel excerpt) with a critical commentary that reflects on the creative process and situates the work in relevant literary and theoretical traditions. This method is particularly suited to students interested in Creative Writing as both practice and scholarship.

### Ethics in research

Ethics approval is required for any research involving human participants — including interviews, surveys, and classroom observation. Key ethical principles include **informed consent** (participants must understand the purpose of the research and agree to participate voluntarily), **confidentiality** (data must be protected and anonymised where necessary), **do no harm** (research must not cause distress or harm to participants), and **integrity** (findings must be reported honestly, without fabrication or falsification). For library-based research, ethical considerations include proper attribution of sources and respect for copyright. Students should keep a research diary to document decisions and reflections throughout the project.

### Proposal writing

A **research proposal** is a detailed plan for the project, typically 2,000–4,000 words. It should include: (1) a title and working title, (2) an introduction stating the research question and its significance, (3) a literature review situating the project in relevant scholarship, (4) a methodology section describing the research design, data collection methods, and analytical framework, (5) a timeline showing key milestones, (6) a bibliography of key sources, and (7) a note on ethical considerations where applicable. The proposal serves as a contract between the student and supervisor, clarifying expectations and providing a framework for feedback.

### Citation and referencing

Academic writing requires consistent and accurate citation. **APA** (American Psychological Association) style is commonly used in the social sciences and education, featuring author-date in-text citations and a reference list. **MLA** (Modern Language Association) style is standard in literary studies, featuring author-page in-text citations and a works cited page. Students should consult the relevant handbook (the *MLA Handbook* for MLA, the *Publication Manual of the American Psychological Association* for APA) and follow their department's preferred style consistently. Proper citation is not merely a technical requirement but an ethical practice that acknowledges intellectual debt and allows readers to trace sources.

### Timeline management

A realistic **timeline** is essential for completing a dissertation within the allocated period. Students should break the project into manageable stages: proposal (weeks 1–4), literature review (weeks 5–10), methodology design and data collection (weeks 11–18), analysis and writing (weeks 19–28), revision and submission (weeks 29–32). Each stage should have clear deliverables and deadlines. Keeping a research diary and a log of meetings with the supervisor helps track progress and identify issues early.

### Supervisor management

The supervisor-student relationship is central to the success of the research project. Students should communicate regularly with their supervisor — at least bi-weekly — and come to meetings prepared with specific questions and progress updates. Supervisors provide guidance on scope, methodology, and academic writing, but the student is responsible for the content and direction of the project. It is important to establish clear expectations at the outset: how often will meetings occur? What format will feedback take? What are the supervisor's areas of expertise and limitation? A productive supervisory relationship is collaborative, not passive; students should actively seek and respond to feedback.

### ESP and Creative Writing connections

**English for Specific Purposes (ESP)** is a viable research topic for the dissertation: students might investigate ESP practices in Kenyan secondary schools, the effectiveness of specific pedagogical approaches for ESP learners, or the linguistic features of ESP genres (academic writing, business English). **Creative Writing** as a research method allows students to explore the relationship between creative practice and critical scholarship, producing a creative-critical dissertation that combines original literary work with analytical reflection. Both approaches expand the range of possible research projects and reflect the programme's commitment to bridging theory and practice.

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

### Short answer

1. Distinguish between library-based, empirical, and creative-critical research methods.
2. What is informed consent, and why is it essential in empirical research?
3. List the key components of a research proposal.
4. What is the difference between APA and MLA citation styles?
5. Why is timeline management important for a dissertation project?
6. Describe the role of the supervisor in a research project.
7. What ethical considerations apply to library-based research?

### Essay questions

8. 'The creative-critical dissertation is a legitimate form of literary scholarship.' Discuss.
9. How should a student manage the relationship with their dissertation supervisor? What makes it productive?
10. Evaluate the strengths and limitations of empirical research methods for a literary studies dissertation.
11. 'Ethics approval is not a bureaucratic hurdle but a fundamental responsibility of the researcher.' Discuss.

### Objective / passage-based

12. (Difficulty: Easy) Which citation style is standard in literary studies: APA or MLA?
13. (Difficulty: Medium) Read the following research question and identify whether it is suitable for a library-based, empirical, or creative-critical project: 'How does the use of free indirect discourse in *Mrs Dalloway* shape the reader's access to Clarissa's inner life?'
14. (Difficulty: Hard) Given a draft research proposal that lacks a clear methodology section, identify the missing elements and rewrite the methodology section for a project on the use of Sheng in Kenyan secondary school classrooms.
