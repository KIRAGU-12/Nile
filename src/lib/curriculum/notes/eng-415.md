## Unit overview

Literary Theory and Criticism introduces students to the major schools of thought that have shaped how we read, interpret, and evaluate literary texts. The unit moves from the structuralist emphasis on underlying systems of meaning through the radical turns of post-structuralism, Marxism, feminism, psychoanalysis, and postcolonial theory, to the diverse approaches of contemporary literary study. Students learn that theory is not an abstract overlay but a set of tools that transforms what we notice in a text and what questions we ask of it.

The unit places particular emphasis on Modern Literary Theory, including post-structuralism, deconstruction, new historicism, cultural materialism, and postmodernism, alongside the earlier schools that laid the groundwork. Students are expected to understand how each theoretical framework arises from specific intellectual and political contexts, and how applying different theories to the same text can yield radically different readings. The unit also encourages students to reflect on their own critical positions and to recognise that reading is never theory-neutral.

By the end of the unit, students should be able to distinguish the central concerns and methods of major theoretical schools, apply theoretical concepts to close readings, and critically evaluate how theory reads texts and vice versa. The unit prepares students for the independent theoretical engagement required in their dissertation and for teaching literature at secondary level with a critical vocabulary.

## Learning outcomes

- Distinguish the central concerns and methods of major theoretical schools from structuralism to postmodernism.
- Apply theoretical concepts (e.g. deconstruction, ideology, performativity, hybridity) to close readings of literary texts.
- Critically evaluate how theory reads texts and how texts challenge theory.
- Compare and contrast Marxist, feminist, and postcolonial approaches to the same text.
- Explain the significance of the 'death of the author' and the 'return of the reader' in literary theory.
- Articulate the relationship between literary theory and cultural context.

## Detailed notes

### Structuralism and the birth of literary theory

**Structuralism**, associated with Claude Lévi-Strauss and Roland Barthes, argues that culture and literature are governed by underlying systems of binary oppositions (nature/culture, raw/cooked, male/female). Meaning arises not from individual texts but from the structures that organise them. Barthes's essay 'The Death of the Author' (1967) declared that the author's intentions and biography are irrelevant; the text and the reader produce meaning. This was a radical shift that placed the act of reading at the centre of literary study.

### Post-structuralism and deconstruction

**Post-structuralism** challenges the structuralist assumption that underlying structures can be definitively mapped. **Deconstruction**, developed by Jacques Derrida, argues that texts contain internal contradictions that undermine their own meanings. The concept of **différance** — the endless deferral of meaning through the play of signifiers — shows that no final, stable interpretation is possible. Derrida's reading of texts reveals how they undermine the binary oppositions they seem to rely on. Foucault's 'archaeology of knowledge' mapped how discourses produce subjects and what counts as truth in different historical periods.

### Marxism and cultural materialism

**Marxist criticism** reads literature as a product of and intervention in material conditions. Drawing on Marx and Engels, it examines how texts reflect or challenge class relations, ideology, and the base/superstructure dynamic. **Cultural materialism** (Raymond Williams, Jonathan Dollimore) extends this by focusing on how literature is embedded in specific material and institutional conditions. A Marxist reading of a novel asks: whose interests does this text serve? How does it naturalise or resist power? The concept of **hegemony** (Gramsci) is key: how do dominant groups maintain cultural authority through seemingly natural arrangements?

### Feminism and gender theory

**Feminist literary criticism** examines how gender is constructed and represented in literature. Simone de Beauvoir's claim that 'one is not born, but rather becomes, a woman' underpins the view that gender is socially constructed. **Écriture féminine** (Hélène Cixous) advocates a feminine writing that escapes patriarchal structures. Judith Butler's theory of **gender performativity** argues that gender is a repeated performance rather than an innate essence. Feminist readings ask whose voices are centred, whose are silenced, and how narratives reinforce or challenge gender norms.

### Psychoanalysis

**Psychoanalytic criticism** draws on Freud's theories of the unconscious, repression, and the Oedipus complex, and Lacan's reworking of these concepts through language. The text is read as a kind of dream-work, with hidden desires and anxieties surfacing through slips, imagery, and narrative structure. The author is treated as an analysand whose unconscious motivations shape the text.

### Postcolonial theory

**Postcolonial theory** analyses the discourse of empire and its legacies. Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978) showed how the West constructed 'the Orient' as exotic, backward, and feminine to justify colonial domination. Frantz Fanon examined the psychological violence of colonialism and the dynamics of decolonisation. Homi Bhabha's concepts of **hybridity** and the **third space** describe the mixed cultural identities that emerge in the contact zone between coloniser and colonised. Gayatri Spivak's 'Can the Subaltern Speak?' interrogates whether the most marginalised can ever be truly represented.

### New historicism and postmodernism

**New historicism** (Stephen Greenblatt) treats literary texts as cultural artefacts embedded in specific historical moments, reading them alongside non-literary documents to reconstruct the 'social energy' of an era. **Postmodernism** in literary theory questions the possibility of objective knowledge, stable identity, and universal truth. It embraces irony, pastiche, and the decentring of the subject, drawing on Derrida, Lyotard (the 'incredulity toward metanarratives'), and Baudrillard (simulacra and hyperreality).

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

### Short answer

1. Explain Barthes's concept of the 'death of the author' and its implications for reading.
2. What does Derrida mean by 'différance'?
3. Define cultural materialism and distinguish it from traditional Marxism.
4. How does Butler's theory of gender performativity challenge essentialist views of gender?
5. What is the 'third space' in Bhabha's theory, and why is it significant for postcolonial studies?
6. Explain how Said's *Orientalism* functions as both a work of scholarship and a critique of empire.

### Essay questions

7. 'Deconstruction does not destroy meaning; it reveals the instability of meaning.' Discuss.
8. Compare Marxist and feminist readings of a single literary text, showing how each foregrounds different aspects of the work.
9. How does new historicism differ from traditional historicism? Use at least one example to illustrate your answer.
10. 'Postmodernism's scepticism toward grand narratives is both liberating and paralysing.' Evaluate this claim.

### Objective / passage-based

11. (Difficulty: Easy) Which theoretical school is most associated with the idea that meaning is produced by the reader rather than the author? (A) Structuralism (B) Deconstruction (C) Marxism (D) New Historicism
12. (Difficulty: Medium) Read the following passage from a postcolonial novel and identify one instance where the text 'writes back' to colonial discourse. Explain your answer.
13. (Difficulty: Hard) Given a passage that contains internal contradictions, apply a deconstructive reading to show how the text undermines its own apparent argument.
