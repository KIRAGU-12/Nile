## Unit overview

Teaching English and Literature in Secondary Schools III is the capstone pedagogy unit for the B.Ed (Arts) in English and Literature programme. It advances the teaching methodologies introduced in earlier years, focusing on curriculum design, the integration of digital tools, the teaching of controversial literature, and professional development for English and Literature teachers. Students learn to design coherent curriculum units across genres and year levels, assess learning effectively, and navigate the challenges of teaching texts that deal with sensitive themes such as gender, violence, and colonialism.

The unit also surfaces the relevance of **English for Specific Purposes (ESP)** — the teaching of English for particular professional or academic contexts — as a dimension of secondary English education, and **Creative Writing** as both a pedagogical approach and a classroom activity. By engaging with curriculum theory (UDL, 5E model, Understanding by Design), ICT tools, and the Kenyan professional development landscape (KELA, action research), students are prepared to enter the classroom as reflective, critically informed practitioners.

By the end of the unit, students should be able to design and assess coherent curriculum units, integrate digital tools appropriately and ethically, teach sensitive literary content with awareness of diverse learners, and articulate a professional development pathway for ongoing growth as an English and Literature teacher.

## Learning outcomes

- Design and assess coherent curriculum units across genres and year levels using established models (5E, UDL, UbD).
- Integrate digital tools and resources appropriately and ethically in the English and Literature classroom.
- Teach sensitive or controversial literary content (e.g. *Things Fall Apart*, texts dealing with gender and violence) with awareness of diverse learners.
- Apply principles of assessment for learning, including formative and summative strategies aligned to learning outcomes.
- Plan a professional development pathway involving KELA membership, peer observation, and action research.
- Incorporate Creative Writing and ESP activities into secondary English lessons.

## Detailed notes

### Curriculum design models

Effective curriculum design begins with clear learning outcomes and a coherent sequence of activities. The **5E model** (Engage, Explore, Explain, Elaborate, Evaluate) provides a constructivist framework for lesson planning: students are first hooked into the topic, then encouraged to investigate, then given explicit explanation, then extended through application, and finally assessed. **Understanding by Design (UbD)**, developed by Wiggins and McTighe, works backwards from desired outcomes: teachers first identify what students should understand, then determine acceptable evidence of understanding, and then plan learning experiences and instruction. **Universal Design for Learning (UDL)** provides a framework for inclusive curriculum design by offering multiple means of engagement, representation, and action/expression, ensuring that all learners can access the curriculum.

### ICT and digital tools

Digital tools can deepen engagement with literature when used purposefully. **Online annotation platforms** (Hypothesis, Perusall) allow students to collaboratively annotate texts, making their thinking visible and fostering peer discussion. **Audio and video texts** — recordings of performances, film adaptations, author interviews — provide multimodal entry points into literary works. **Digital storytelling** tools enable students to create their own responses to literature, developing both creative and critical skills. However, digital integration requires teaching **digital literacy** and critical evaluation of sources. Teachers must model how to assess the reliability of online information and use technology ethically.

### Teaching controversial literature

Some texts — Chinua Achebe's *Things Fall Apart*, novels dealing with gender-based violence, colonialism, and other sensitive themes — can trigger strong responses in learners. Establishing **ground rules** for discussion is essential: students should feel safe to express views but also learn to listen respectfully and engage with perspectives different from their own. Where school policy allows, **opt-outs** should be available for students who find particular content distressing. Teachers should connect texts to learners' lived experience carefully, avoiding re-traumatisation while still enabling critical engagement. The goal is not to avoid difficult content but to teach students how to think critically about it.

### Assessment

Assessment in the English and Literature classroom should be **balanced** across reading, writing, speaking, and listening, and aligned to learning outcomes. **Formative assessment** (ongoing, informal checks for understanding — quizzes, discussion, exit tickets) informs instruction in real time. **Summative assessment** (end-of-unit tasks, examinations, portfolios) evaluates cumulative learning. **Assessment for learning** involves students in the process: self-assessment, peer feedback, and rubrics that make criteria transparent. Creative and alternative assessments — such as creative writing responses, oral presentations, and digital projects — can capture dimensions of learning that traditional tests miss.

### Professional development

Sustained professional growth is essential for English and Literature teachers. **KELA** (Kenya English Language Association) and **KAT** (Kenya Association of Teachers) provide communities of practice where teachers share resources, discuss challenges, and stay current with scholarship. **Action research** — systematic inquiry into one's own teaching practice — is a powerful form of professional development that connects theory to classroom reality. Teachers can investigate questions such as how digital tools affect student engagement with literature, or how creative writing supports critical reading. Peer observation and mentoring are additional strategies for growth.

### ESP and Creative Writing in the secondary classroom

**English for Specific Purposes (ESP)** has a place in the secondary English classroom, particularly in the senior years where students prepare for academic and professional contexts. ESP activities — formal letter writing, report writing, academic essay structure — can be integrated into literature lessons by asking students to write analytical essays, character profiles, or contextual notes. **Creative Writing** is both a pedagogical approach and a classroom activity: writing poetry, fiction, and drama in response to literary texts deepens students' understanding of craft, voice, and form. Creative writing tasks can also serve as assessment, allowing students to demonstrate understanding through production rather than only analysis.

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

### Short answer

1. Describe the 5E model and explain how each phase supports student learning.
2. What is Understanding by Design (UbD), and how does it differ from forward-design curriculum planning?
3. Define Universal Design for Learning (UDL) and list its three principles.
4. Why is it important to establish ground rules before teaching controversial literature?
5. What is action research, and how does it contribute to teacher professional development?
6. Give one example of how ESP activities can be integrated into a literature lesson.
7. Explain the difference between formative and summative assessment.

### Essay questions

8. 'The best English literature teachers use a range of curriculum design models to meet diverse learner needs.' Discuss.
9. How can digital tools enhance the teaching of controversial literature? What are the risks, and how can they be managed?
10. Evaluate the role of KELA and professional associations in supporting English teachers in Kenya.
11. 'Creative Writing should be a central component of the secondary English curriculum.' Discuss.

### Objective / passage-based

12. (Difficulty: Easy) Which curriculum model works backwards from desired outcomes to plan instruction? (A) 5E model (B) UbD (C) UDL (D) Direct instruction
13. (Difficulty: Medium) Read the following classroom scenario and identify which UDL principle the teacher is applying: a teacher provides a text in both print and audio format.
14. (Difficulty: Hard) Given a lesson plan that integrates a digital annotation tool with a controversial text, evaluate the plan's alignment with the learning outcomes and identify one strength and one area for improvement.
