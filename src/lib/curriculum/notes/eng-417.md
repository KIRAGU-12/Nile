## Unit overview

Postcolonial Literature examines the theories and texts of writing that emerges from the experience of colonialism, migration, and cultural encounter. The unit traces how writers from formerly colonised regions have used the language of the coloniser — English — to articulate their own histories, identities, and aspirations. It explores the key concepts that define the field: orientalism, mimicry, hybridity, diaspora, the subaltern, and the strategy of 'writing back' to empire. Students learn to read postcolonial texts with a critical vocabulary that illuminates the power dynamics embedded in literary production and reception.

The unit also surfaces Comparative Literature angles, considering how postcolonial writing in English relates to literary traditions in other languages, how translation and migration shape literary production, and how the canon of English literature has been expanded and challenged by postcolonial voices. Writers studied include Chinua Achebe, V.S. Naipaul, Salman Rushdie, Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie, and Jhumpa Lahiri, among others.

By the end of the unit, students should be able to apply postcolonial concepts to texts, analyse how writers write back to empire and represent cultural translation, and interpret literature's role in decolonisation and identity formation. The unit prepares students for the independent theoretical engagement required in their dissertation and for teaching postcolonial literature in secondary schools.

## Learning outcomes

- Apply postcolonial concepts (orientalism, hybridity, mimicry, subaltern) to literary texts.
- Analyse how writers write back to empire and represent cultural translation.
- Interpret literature's role in decolonisation and identity formation.
- Distinguish between orientalism as a discourse and as a literary strategy.
- Explain the significance of mimicry and hybridity in postcolonial writing.
- Compare postcolonial writing in English with literary traditions from other linguistic contexts (Comparative Literature).

## Detailed notes

### Orientalism and the imperial archive

Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978) argued that the West did not simply 'discover' the East but constructed it — as sensual, backward, despotic, and irrational — in order to justify colonial domination. **Orientalism** is both a scholarly tradition (Western studies of the East that treat Eastern cultures as exotic and static) and a discourse that shapes how the East is represented in literature, art, and politics. Said showed that the Orient is not a natural fact but a construction that serves Western imperial interests. Postcolonial literature challenges this construction by representing Eastern experiences from the inside, disrupting the Western gaze.

### Writing back to empire

**Writing back** is the strategy by which colonial and subordinate writers re-articulate themselves in the coloniser's language. Chinua Achebe's *Things Fall Apart* (1958) is the touchstone text: it tells the Igbo encounter with British colonialism from the inside, presenting Igbo culture with complexity and dignity before showing its destruction. The novel's title ironises the collapse of traditional order — 'Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold' — and its narrative voice refuses the condescension of earlier colonial representations. V.S. Naipaul's work offers a more ambivalent 'writing back', critiquing both colonialism and the postcolonial condition. **Mastery of the coloniser's language** — Achebe's concept — means using English not as a colonised tongue but as a tool of resistance and self-definition.

### Hybridity and mimicry

Homi Bhabha's concepts of **hybridity** and **mimicry** are central to postcolonial theory. **Hybridity** names the mixed cultural identities that emerge in the contact zone between coloniser and colonised — identities that are neither one nor the other but forged in the **third space** of cultural negotiation. **Mimicry** is the colonised subject's partial imitation of the coloniser that almost matches but never quite — 'almost the same, but not quite' — producing a threat to colonial authority because it exposes the instability of the colonial gaze. Mimicry is not mere imitation but a form of resistance that unsettles the binary between coloniser and colonised.

### The subaltern and representation

The concept of the **subaltern**, drawn from Gramsci and developed by Gayatri Spivak, refers to the most marginalised members of society — those who are excluded from dominant discourses of power. Spivak's essay 'Can the Subaltern Speak?' (1988) asks whether the voiceless can ever be truly represented, and warns that even well-intentioned representation can silence the subaltern by speaking for them. Postcolonial literature must navigate this tension: how to give voice to the marginalised without appropriating or distorting their experience.

### Diaspora and the journey narrative

**Diaspora** refers to the scattering of a people from their homeland and the cultural productions that emerge from this displacement. Postcolonial literature tracks the **journey narrative** — the movement across borders, the experience of translation and cultural re-creation in a new land. Writers such as Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie (*Americanah*), Jhumpa Lahiri (*The Namesake*), and Dinaw Mengestu (*The Beautiful Things That Heaven Bears*) explore how migrants negotiate identity between cultures, how language shifts and hybridises, and how the past is remembered and reinvented in a new context. The diaspora experience is not simply one of loss but of creative transformation.

### Comparative Literature dimensions

Comparative Literature in this unit involves reading postcolonial writing in English alongside works from other linguistic and national traditions. Students should consider how Achebe's *Things Fall Apart* relates to African oral traditions, how Rushdie's magical realism connects to Latin American literature, how Adichie's novels dialogue with the American literary canon, and how translation and migration shape literary production across borders. The unit encourages students to think beyond the national literature framework and to consider how English-language postcolonial writing participates in global literary conversations.

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

### Short answer

1. Define orientalism as described by Edward Said, and explain how it functions as both a discourse and a literary strategy.
2. What does 'writing back to empire' mean? Illustrate with one text.
3. Explain Bhabha's concept of mimicry and its relationship to colonial authority.
4. What is the 'third space' in postcolonial theory, and why is it significant?
5. How does Spivak's question 'Can the Subaltern Speak?' challenge postcolonial representation?
6. Define hybridity and give one example from a postcolonial text.
7. What is the journey narrative, and how does it appear in postcolonial literature?

### Essay questions

8. 'Achebe's *Things Fall Apart* is a foundational text of postcolonial writing because it writes back to the colonial narrative.' Discuss.
9. Compare the treatment of cultural identity in two postcolonial texts, using the concepts of hybridity and mimicry.
10. How does diaspora shape the form and content of postcolonial literature? Use at least two examples.
11. 'Orientalism continues to shape how the West represents the East in literature.' Evaluate this claim with reference to at least one text.

### Objective / passage-based

12. (Difficulty: Easy) Which concept describes the colonised subject's partial imitation of the coloniser that 'almost the same, but not quite'? (A) Hybridity (B) Mimicry (C) Subaltern (D) Orientalism
13. (Difficulty: Medium) Read the following passage from a postcolonial novel and identify one instance of 'writing back' to colonial discourse. Explain your answer.
14. (Difficulty: Hard) Given a passage that blends the coloniser's language with the colonised culture's idioms, analyse how the text enacts hybridity and what this reveals about cultural identity.
