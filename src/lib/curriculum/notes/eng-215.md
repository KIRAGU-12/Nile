## Unit overview

Old English (Anglo-Saxon) literature spans roughly the fifth century to the Norman Conquest of 1066, during which the earliest surviving works of English poetry were composed and transmitted orally before being written down by monastic scribes. The corpus is dominated by heroic and Christian verse, the most celebrated example being *Beowulf*, an epic of nearly 3,200 lines that brings together the Germanic warrior ethos and Christian theology in a single, enduring narrative. Understanding this literature requires familiarity with the social world of the early medieval mead-hall, the oral-formulaic techniques of the scop, and the dual value system that shaped Anglo-Saxon identity.

The period's poetry is characterised by a distinctive metrical system — accentual verse — in which four stressed syllables per line, linked by alliteration, replace the quantitative metre of classical Latin. This system, together with the kenning (a compound metaphor such as 'whale-road' for sea) and the elegiac tone that pervades so much of the poetry, gives Old English literature its unique texture. The Christian conversion of the Anglo-Saxons from the sixth century onwards added a layer of theological interpretation that transforms pagan heroic material into a meditation on mortality, sin and salvation.

Studying this Unit equips students with the analytical tools to read Old English poetry in translation and to recognise the formal devices — alliteration, variation, litotes — that define the period. It also situates medieval English literature within the broader European context of migration, conversion and the transmission of classical learning through monastic networks, providing a foundation for the study of Middle English and Chaucer in the companion Unit ENG 216.

## Learning outcomes

- Identify the formal features of Old English verse, including alliteration, the caesura and the four-stress line.
- Define and apply key terms such as kenning, wyrd, comitatus and litotes in close analysis of passages.
- Explain the tension between the Germanic heroic code and Christian values in *Beowulf*.
- Analyse the role of the scop and the oral-formulaic tradition in the composition of Old English poetry.
- Evaluate the significance of the Christian conversion for the transformation of heroic literature.
- Compare the thematic concerns of Old English poetry with those of the early medieval period that follows.

## Detailed notes

### Old English metre and poetic diction

Old English verse is accentual rather than syllabic: each line contains four stressed syllables divided by a medial pause or caesura. The two half-lines are linked by alliteration, in which the initial consonant sound of the first stressed syllable in the second half-line echoes the alliterating sound of one or both stressed syllables in the first half-line. This system, inherited from Germanic oral tradition, relies on formulaic phrases and variation — the same idea expressed in different words — which aids memorisation and performance. The poetic vocabulary includes many compound words and archaic terms that give the verse its dense, allusive quality. In Kenyan classrooms, teachers can use this system to help students appreciate that English verse has Germanic roots distinct from the Romance tradition, a point of pride in a multilingual context where English coexists with Kiswahili and over sixty other languages.

### Kennings and figurative language

A kenning is a two-word metaphor that replaces a plain noun: 'whale-road' for sea, 'battle-sweat' for blood, 'ring-giver' for king, 'whale-path' for ocean. Kennings draw on the Anglo-Saxon world of seafaring, warfare and kinship, compressing complex images into compact form. They function as a kind of poetic code that rewards the attentive listener or reader. Other figurative devices include litotes (understatement, as in 'not the least good' to mean very good) and the use of epithets and kennings in combination to build a layered poetic texture. In Kenyan literary education, kennings offer an excellent entry point for teaching figurative language, since students in a multilingual setting are already accustomed to metaphorical thinking across languages — for instance, Sheng expressions that compress complex social meanings into single slang terms.

### The heroic code and combatting

The heroic code of Anglo-Saxon society centred on loyalty, courage, honour and revenge. The bond between lord and thane (*comitatus*) was the fundamental social contract: the thane pledged loyalty and military service in exchange for protection and the sharing of treasure. Combatting was the arena in which a warrior proved his worth; glory (*mærþ*) won in battle was the only immortality available to a pagan warrior. The poem *Beowulf* stages combat in three episodes — against Grendel, Grendel's mother, and the dragon — each escalating in stakes and each testing the hero's adherence to the code. The combat scenes also illustrate the poet's use of cataloguing and imagery drawn from weapons, armour and the natural world, techniques that students can practise identifying in close reading.

### Beowulf: themes and structure

*Beowulf* is the longest and most important surviving Old English poem, composed in the late first millennium and preserved in the Nowell Codex (c. 1000). The poem follows the hero from his youthful exploits in Denmark (killing Grendel and his mother) to his death as an aged Geatish king fighting a dragon. Its major themes include the heroic code versus Christian morality, the inevitability of fate (*wyrd*), the fragility of the mead-hall as a centre of civilisation, and the tension between earthly fame and spiritual salvation. The poem's Christian scribe frames the pagan hero within a salvation history, creating a rich ambiguity that has generated centuries of critical debate. For Kenyan students, the theme of the hero's journey — from youth to kingship to death — resonates with oral traditions in many Kenyan communities, offering a point of comparative engagement.

### Christian conversion and the poet's lens

The Anglo-Saxon conversion to Christianity, beginning in the late sixth century, transformed the literary landscape. Many Old English poems are the work of Christian scribes who reinterpreted pagan material through a Christian lens: Grendel is described as a descendant of Cain, the dragon episode echoes the Fall of Adam, and the funeral of Beowulf invokes Christian burial rites. The tension between the pagan heroic world (fate, revenge, glory) and Christian values (humility, mercy, hope of salvation) is the poem's enduring power. Poets like Cynewulf (fl. 9th–10th c.) wrote explicitly Christian poetry — *The Dream of Christ*, *Elene* — using the same alliterative verse form to explore spiritual themes. The conversion narrative also mirrors the experience of Kenyan communities where indigenous and Christian world-views coexist and interpenetrate, a dynamic that can enrich classroom discussion.

### Wyrd, comitatus and the Anglo-Saxon world-view

*Wyrd* (fate) is the inexorable web that governs human life; even the greatest hero cannot escape it. This concept shapes the elegiac tone of much Old English poetry, in which the transience of worldly glory is mourned. *Comitatus* (the lord-retainer bond) is the ethical core of the heroic world: its violation — by a thane who flees battle, for example — is the deepest shame. The mead-hall (*heorot*) symbolises civilisation, warmth and community, while the wilderness outside represents chaos and death. These concepts — wyrd, comitatus, the hall-as-world — define the Anglo-Saxon world-view and recur throughout the literature of the period. In Kenyan literary studies, these ideas can be compared with communal values in African oral traditions, where the elder's role as protector and the community's expectation of loyalty echo the comitatus bond.

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

### Short answer (moderate)
1. Define the term 'kenning' and give two examples from *Beowulf* or other Old English poetry.
2. Explain how alliteration functions as the structural glue of the Old English half-line.
3. What is *wyrd*, and how does it shape the tragic dimension of *Beowulf*?
4. Distinguish between the heroic code and Christian values as they appear in *Beowulf*.
5. What is the *comitatus* bond, and what happens when it is broken?

### Essay questions (challenging)
1. 'The tension between the pagan heroic code and Christian values is the defining feature of Old English literature.' Discuss with reference to *Beowulf*.
2. How does the Old English poet use kennings and alliteration to create a distinctive poetic world? Analyse at least two examples in detail.
3. Compare the role of the mead-hall in *Beowulf* with its significance in the broader Anglo-Saxon poetic imagination.

### Objective / passage-based (moderate to challenging)
1. Identify the alliterating sounds in the following half-lines from *Beowulf* (trans. Heaney): 'The monster stirred, that demon walking / Down the dark miles of the dream-swamp.'
2. Which of the following is a kenning? (a) 'the lord's ring' (b) 'the brave warrior' (c) 'the sea's wave' (d) 'the king's sword'. Explain your choice.
3. In the passage from *The Wanderer*, the speaker mourns the loss of his lord and the dissolution of the comitatus bond. What does this reveal about Anglo-Saxon values?
4. Multiple choice: The caesura in Old English verse divides the line into (a) two unstressed half-lines (b) two half-lines, one stressed and one unstressed (c) two half-lines separated by a medial pause (d) four equal stresses.
