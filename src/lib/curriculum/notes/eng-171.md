## Unit overview

Introduction to Poetry orients students to the distinctive craft of verse: how meaning, emotion and music are made through line, rhythm, sound, imagery and figurative language. The unit covers metrical patterns, rhyme schemes, stanza forms and the sonnet tradition, while also exploring free verse and contemporary experimental poetry. For Kenyan students, the unit provides a framework for reading poems from diverse traditions — European, African, Caribbean — and for recognising how poetic form shapes meaning. It also connects poetry to oral performance traditions, from praise poetry to spoken word, validating Kenyan poetic culture within a global literary history.

By the end of the semester, students should be able to scan a poem for metre, identify sound devices and figurative language, and write analytical paragraphs that show how form and technique produce meaning. These skills are directly examined in the KCSE Literature Paper 2 and in university coursework, where questions frequently require detailed commentary on a poem's diction, imagery and structure.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this unit, students should be able to:
- Read poetry aloud to hear and identify its rhythm, metre and sound patterns.
- Identify and analyse key poetic devices: metre, rhyme, imagery, metaphor, symbol, personification.
- Interpret how form and technique shape meaning across poetic traditions.
- Compare formal features of different poems, including the sonnet, ballad, ode and free verse.
- Analyse Kenyan and African poems in relation to oral performance traditions.
- Write structured analytical essays that support interpretive claims with close quotation.

## Detailed notes

### Form, line and sound

A **line** is the basic unit of poetry; a **stanza** is its paragraph. **Metre** is the pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables: iambic (unstressed-STRESSED), trochaic (STRESSED-unstressed), anapestic and dactylic. **Rhyme schemes** are labelled with letters. **Sound devices** include alliteration (repeated initial consonants), assonance (repeated vowel sounds), consonance (repeated consonants) and onomatopoeia. **Enjambment** — running a sentence across a line break — controls pace and surprise; a caesura — a pause within a line — creates emphasis or tension.

### Figurative language and meaning

Poetry works through density and indirection. A **metaphor** states one thing is another; a **simile** uses 'like' or 'as'. A **symbol** carries meaning beyond itself — a dove, a road, a season. **Imagery** appeals to the senses. Because poetry compresses, a single image can carry thematic weight. The **speaker** of a lyric is rarely the poet; always ask what the persona's attitude is and what dramatic situation the poem implies.

### The sonnet and fixed forms

The sonnet is a fourteen-line form with two chief kinds: **Petrarchan** (octave presents a problem, sestet replies) and **Shakespearean** (three quatrains + final couplet). The **volta** — the 'turn' of argument or emotion — usually comes at the ninth line or the final couplet. Other fixed forms include the **ballad** (narrative, song-like, often ABAB or ABCB rhyme), the **ode** (celebratory, elevated) and the **elegy** (mourning). **Free verse** rejects regular metre and rhyme but retains attention to line, rhythm and visual shape.

### Poetry and orality in Kenya

Kenyan poetry sits at the intersection of oral tradition and written form. Praise poetry (*bĩrĩku* among the Kalenjin, *maua* among the Kamba) uses repetition, call-and-response and formulaic language. Contemporary Kenyan poets — Okello wa Lande-Liage, Micere Githae Mugo, Binyavanga Wainaina — adapt these techniques to the page and to performance. Understanding this lineage helps students read African poems not as imitations of European models but as distinct artistic traditions with their own formal conventions.

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

**Short answer**
1. Scan the following line of poetry for metre and identify the dominant metrical pattern: "Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?" (Medium)
2. Define "enjambment" and explain its effect in one poem you have studied. (Medium)
3. How does the volta function in a Petrarchan sonnet? (Easy)

**Essay questions**
1. "Poetry compresses meaning; every word counts." Discuss with reference to two poems studied this semester. (Medium)
2. Analyse how metre, rhyme and imagery work together to create emotional effect in one poem. (Medium)
3. "African poetry is rooted in oral tradition but transformed by the written word." Discuss with reference to at least two Kenyan or African poems. (Hard)

**Objective / passage-based**
1. Read the following poem extract and answer the questions that follow. (Hard)
   a. Identify the rhyme scheme and comment on its effect. (2 marks)
   b. Find and analyse two examples of figurative language. (4 marks)
   c. How does the poet's use of enjambment affect the poem's pace? (2 marks)
