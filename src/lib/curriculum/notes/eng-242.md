## Unit overview

Romantic Literature traces the revolution in poetry and prose that began with the publication of *Lyrical Ballads* in 1798 and extended through the early nineteenth century. The Romantics reacted against Enlightenment rationalism and the industrial transformation of society, exalting the imagination, nature, emotion, the individual and the supernatural. Wordsworth's declaration that poetry is the 'spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings' and Coleridge's concept of the 'esemplastic' imagination — the mind's power to shape and unify experience — reorganised the understanding of what poetry could do. The period also saw the emergence of the gothic novel, the Byronic hero, the ode as a major lyric form, and new theories of the sublime that linked aesthetic experience to the overwhelming power of nature.

The Romantics were deeply engaged with the political upheavals of their age, particularly the French Revolution and its aftermath. Wordsworth's early radicalism and his later conservatism, Shelley's political idealism, Byron's cynical scepticism — these reflect the period's shifting responses to revolution, industrialisation and the rise of empire. The Romantics also developed a new theory of the poet as a visionary figure, a 'national singer' in Wordsworth's phrase, whose imaginative power sets the literature apart from the prose of the Enlightenment. This Unit introduces students to the major Romantic poets and texts, the key concepts of Romantic theory, and the historical contexts that shaped the period's literary production.

The Unit builds on the Renaissance foundations of ENG 231 and prepares students for the development of literary criticism in ENG 201. Students will learn to analyse Romantic poetry with attention to form, imagery and theory, and to situate Romantic writing within the broader intellectual and political currents of the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries.

## Learning outcomes

- Trace the main themes and concerns of British Romanticism.
- Analyse the use of nature, imagination and the supernatural in major poems.
- Situate Romantic writing against the French Revolution and industrialisation.
- Explain the concept of the sublime and its role in Romantic poetics.
- Identify and analyse the Byronic hero as a literary archetype.
- Evaluate Keats's concept of negative capability and Shelley's defence of poetry.

## Detailed notes

### Lyrical Ballads and the revolution in poetics

*Lyrical Ballads* (1798), a collaboration between Wordsworth and Coleridge, launched English Romanticism. Wordsworth's Preface to the second edition (1800) declared that poetry is the 'spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings' and that the language of ordinary men is the fittest medium for poetry. This was a radical claim: it rejected the elevated diction of the Augustan tradition and asserted that the everyday experiences of common life — the loves, losses and labours of rural people — were worthy poetic subjects. Coleridge's contribution, including 'The Rime of the Ancient Mariner' and 'Kubla Khan', brought the supernatural and the imaginative into the collection. The Preface and the collection together represent a manifesto for a new kind of poetry, one grounded in personal experience and imaginative vision.

### Nature as moral and spiritual force

For the Romantics, nature was not merely scenery but a living, moral force — 'a presence full of love' (Wordsworth). Nature heals the wounded spirit, teaches truth and provides a counterweight to the alienation of industrial society. Wordsworth's 'Tintern Abbey' (1798) traces the poet's return to a landscape he knew as a youth, exploring how nature's restorative power deepens with age and experience. Coleridge's 'Frost at Midnight' imagines nature as a teacher of 'the secret ministry of frost'. Shelley's 'Mont Blanc' confronts the terrifying power of the natural world and asks whether nature's 'voice' is a 'still, sad music of humanity'. The Romantics' engagement with nature was both personal and philosophical, and it reflected a broader cultural shift away from urban industrial life toward the rural and the wild.

### Imagination and the creative mind

Coleridge distinguished between 'fancy' — the mechanical arrangement of materials — and 'imagination' — the esemplastic power that unifies perception and creates new wholes. Wordsworth's imagination was the faculty that transformed ordinary experience into poetic vision; in 'The Prelude', the epic poem of his life, memory and imagination work together to shape the raw material of experience into meaningful form. Shelley's defence of poetry in *A Defence of Poetry* (1821, published 1840) argues that the poet is the 'unacknowledged legislator of the world', whose imagination creates the symbols through which society understands itself. The Romantic emphasis on imagination marked a decisive shift from the Enlightenment's valuation of reason and observation to a new centrality for the creative mind.

### The sublime and the gothic

The sublime — awe mixed with terror in the face of vast natural power — is central to Romantic aesthetics. Edmund Burke's *A Philosophical Enquiry into the Origin of Our Ideas of the Sublime and Beautiful* (1757) defined the sublime as the experience of overwhelming vastness, obscurity or power that produces a thrilling mixture of fear and delight. Kant's 'dynamical sublime' emphasised the mind's capacity to overcome the terror of nature through the exercise of moral reason. The gothic novel, which emerged in the late eighteenth century, explored the psychological dimensions of the sublime: Ann Radcliffe's haunted castles, Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein* (1818), which takes the gothic into the modern age with Victor's Promethean ambition and the Creature's education and rage. The gothic and the sublime are intertwined in Romantic literature, both exploring the limits of human experience in the face of overwhelming forces.

### The Byronic hero

The Byronic hero — defiant, melancholy, rebellious against society — is the archetypal Romantic figure, named after Lord Byron's narrative poems *Childe Harold's Pilgrimage* and *Don Juan*. The Byronic hero is a figure of extraordinary talent and passion who is alienated from society by his own nature, often haunted by a dark secret or a past transgression. Byron's hero combines personal charisma with social critique, and the figure influenced the Romantic generation and beyond: Emily Brontë's Heathcliff, Melville's Ahab, and even modern fictional antiheroes descend from the Byronic tradition. Students should learn to identify the Byronic hero as a literary archetype and to analyse how specific texts use or subvert the conventions of the type.

### Odes: Keats, Shelley and the ode tradition

The ode became a major lyric form in the Romantic period, offering a flexible structure for meditation on a single subject. Keats's odes — 'Ode on a Grecian Urn', 'Ode to a Nightingale', 'To Autumn' — are among the finest achievements of English poetry. 'Ode on a Grecian Urn' meditates on art, permanence and desire; the famous closing — 'Beauty is truth, truth beauty' — has sparked endless debate about whether Keats endorses an aesthetic of pure beauty or exposes the limits of art to know truth. Shelley's odes — 'Ode to the West Wind', 'Ode to a Skylark' — celebrate the transformative power of nature and the poet's imaginative identification with natural forces. The ode form, with its strophic structure and its movement from description to meditation, suited the Romantic preoccupation with the relationship between the individual mind and the larger world.

### Keats's negative capability

Negative capability is Keats's concept for the poet's ability to remain in 'uncertainties, mysteries, doubts, without any irritable reaching after fact and reason'. It is the capacity to dwell in the unknown without forcing resolution, to accept paradox and contradiction as inherent to human experience. Keats developed the concept in letters to his brothers, and it has become one of the most influential ideas in Romantic literary criticism. Negative capability is evident in Keats's odes, where the poems do not resolve their tensions — between beauty and mortality, permanence and change, art and life — but hold them in productive balance. For students, negative capability offers a way to read Romantic poetry not as a search for answers but as a sustained engagement with questions that resist easy resolution.

### Shelley's defence of poetry

Shelley's *A Defence of Poetry* (1821, published 1840) is one of the most important Romantic statements on the nature and value of poetry. Shelley argues that poetry is not mere ornament but a fundamental human activity: the poet creates the symbols through which society understands itself, and the poet is 'the unacknowledged legislator of the world'. Shelley defends poetry against the charge that it is impractical and idle, arguing that poetry is the vehicle through which moral and social progress is imagined and communicated. Shelley's defence also reflects his own radical political commitments: poetry, for Shelley, is inseparable from the struggle for freedom and justice. Students should read *A Defence of Poetry* alongside Keats's letters and the Preface to *Lyrical Ballads* to trace the Romantic development of poetic theory.

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

### Short answer (moderate)
1. What was the significance of *Lyrical Ballads* (1798) for the Romantic movement?
2. Explain Coleridge's distinction between fancy and imagination.
3. Define the sublime as understood by Burke and Kant.
4. What is the Byronic hero, and how does Byron's Childe Harold exemplify this type?
5. Explain Keats's concept of negative capability with reference to one of his odes.
6. What does Shelley mean by calling the poet 'the unacknowledged legislator of the world'?

### Essay questions (challenging)
1. 'Romantic nature poetry is not about scenery but about the relationship between the mind and the natural world.' Discuss with reference to Wordsworth and at least one other Romantic poet.
2. How does Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein* use the gothic to explore the consequences of unchecked ambition? Analyse the novel's framing structure and its central themes.
3. Compare Keats's 'Ode on a Grecian Urn' with Shelley's 'Ode to the West Wind'. How do the two poems differ in their treatment of permanence and change?

### Objective / passage-based (moderate to challenging)
1. The Preface to *Lyrical Ballads* was written by (a) Coleridge (b) Wordsworth (c) Byron (d) Shelley.
2. Which of the following best describes negative capability? (a) the ability to write quickly (b) the capacity to dwell in uncertainties without reaching for facts (c) a technique for writing sonnets (d) a form of Romantic satire.
3. Passage analysis: Read the opening lines of Wordsworth's 'Tintern Abbey' and identify two features of Romantic nature poetry.
4. Multiple choice: *Frankenstein* was written by (a) Ann Radcliffe (b) Mary Shelley (c) Lord Byron (d) John Keats.
