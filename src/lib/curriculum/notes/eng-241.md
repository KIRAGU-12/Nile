## Unit overview

Restoration and Eighteenth-Century Literature examines the rise of neoclassicism, the heroic couplet, and the drama of manners in a period of profound cultural transformation. After the Puritan closure of the theatres in 1642 and the Interregnum, the Restoration of the monarchy in 1660 reopened playhouses and unleashed a new, secular, urban culture of wit and pleasure. Literature turned to the city, to reason, to order, and the neoclassical ideals of decorum, imitation of nature and the three unities shaped the period's literary production. The heroic couplet — closed rhyming pairs of iambic pentameter — became the dominant verse form, perfected by Dryden and Pope as the vehicle for epigram, satire and didacticism.

Satire as a mode attacks folly and vice through ridicule, and the eighteenth century produced some of the greatest satirical works in English: Swift's *Gulliver's Travels* and *A Modest Proposal*, Pope's *The Rape of the Lock* and *An Essay on Man*. Restoration comedy of manners — Wycherley's *The Country Wife*, Congreve's *The Way of the World* — exposes sexual manners and social hypocrisy through witty, often amoral dialogue. The period also saw the emergence of the novel and the rise of periodical essay-writing (Addison and Steele's *The Spectator*), reflecting the growing importance of the reading public and the culture of politeness and reason.

This Unit equips students to identify neoclassical ideals, analyse the heroic couplet and Augustan poetic technique, and interpret the social satire of the period. It also introduces the concept of the Golden Age of English satire and the ways in which Restoration and eighteenth-century literature reflects — and critiques — the social hierarchies of its time.

## Learning outcomes

- Identify the neoclassical ideals of order, decorum and imitation of nature.
- Analyse the heroic couplet and Augustan poetic technique in Pope and Dryden.
- Interpret the social satire of Restoration comedy and Swift's mock-heroic.
- Explain the significance of the three unities in neoclassical drama.
- Evaluate the role of wit and reason in eighteenth-century literary culture.
- Compare Restoration comedy of manners with later satirical modes.

## Detailed notes

### Neoclassicism and the three unities

Neoclassicism imposed rules drawn from Aristotle and classical antiquity: the three unities of time (the action should occur within a single day), place (a single location) and action (a single coherent plot). These rules were intended to produce order, unity and decorum — the idea that each genre observes its own level of language and subject matter. Dryden and Pope adapted these principles to English contexts, using them to judge literary works and to guide their own writing. The neoclassical ideal was that literature should instruct and delight, combining moral purpose with aesthetic pleasure. This emphasis on order and reason reflected the broader intellectual currents of the Enlightenment, which valued rationality, empiricism and the systematic study of human nature.

### The heroic couplet and Augustan verse

The heroic couplet — a pair of rhyming iambic pentameter lines, usually closed (each line is a complete syntactic unit) — became the characteristic verse form of the Augustan age. Pope's couplets are polished, epigrammatic and balanced: 'True ease in writing comes from art, not chance, / As those move lightest who have learned to dance' (from *Epistle to Burlington*). Dryden used the couplet for both poetry and drama, lending it a flexibility that made it suitable for satire, translation and philosophical reflection. The couplet's balance and antithesis mirror the period's taste for reason, wit and intellectual elegance. Students should learn to analyse how the closed couplet creates a sense of resolution and how enjambment across couplet lines can disrupt this effect.

### Satire: modes and masters

Satire attacks folly, vice and folly through ridicule, irony and exaggeration. The Augustan age produced several modes of satire: the Horatian satire (gentle, witty, amused) and the Juvenalian satire (biting, harsh, morally indignant). Swift's *A Modest Proposal* (1729) is the supreme example of Juvenalian satire and mock-heroic: it treats the horrific suggestion that Irish parents sell their children as food in a rational, measured, economic tone, exposing English cruelty through the very rationality of its argument. The juxtaposition of lofty style and base content is the engine of the satire. Pope's *The Rape of the Lock* uses mock-heroic to treat a trivial social incident — a stolen lock of hair — with the grandeur of epic, thereby satirising the vanity of the upper classes.

### Restoration comedy of manners

Restoration comedy — the drama that flourished after 1660 — exposes the sexual manners and social hypocrisies of the court and the city. Wycherley's *The Country Wife* (1675) uses innuendo and double entendre to expose the sexual anxieties of the upper classes; Congreve's *The Way of the World* (1700) deploys polished, witty dialogue to explore the intersection of love, money and social reputation. These plays are characterised by their cynical view of marriage as an economic transaction, their celebration of sexual wit, and their exposure of the gap between social codes and actual desires. The comedy of manners reflects the Restoration court's culture of licence and its anxiety about moral decay — anxieties that the comedies themselves both exploit and critique.

### The Augustan age and the Golden Age of satire

The term 'Golden Age' of English satire refers to the remarkable concentration of satirical talent in the early eighteenth century: Pope, Swift, Dryden, Johnson, Gay and Addison. This was a period of intense literary activity, supported by a growing patronage system and a new public of literate readers. The satirists of this age were united by their commitment to reason, their distrust of enthusiasm and their belief that literature should improve society. Pope's *An Essay on Man* (1709–34) sets out the neoclassical world-view in the famous line 'Whatever IS, is right' — a theodicy of order that has been both celebrated and critiqued. The Augustan satirists also pioneered the use of the periodical essay and the mock-heroic, forms that would influence literature for centuries.

### Pope, Swift and the rational tradition

Alexander Pope (1688–1744) was the dominant Augustan poet, famous for his polished heroic couplets and satirical precision. His *Essay on Criticism* (1711) articulates neoclassical principles of literary judgment; *The Rape of the Lock* (1712, 1714) satirises the trivial vanities of the upper classes; *An Essay on Man* (1733–34) explores the place of humanity in the cosmic order. Jonathan Swift (1667–1745), the Anglo-Irish satirist and clergyman, directed his savage wit against English policy in Ireland and human folly. *Gulliver's Travels* (1726) uses the travel narrative to satirise human nature at every level — from the petty rivalries of Lilliput to the rational excesses of the Houyhnhnms. Swift's *A Modest Proposal* (1729) remains one of the most devastating satires in English, a text that every student of literature must learn to read for its layered ironies.

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

### Short answer (moderate)
1. Define the three unities and explain their neoclassical significance.
2. What is the heroic couplet, and why did it become the dominant verse form of the Augustan age?
3. Distinguish between Horatian and Juvenalian satire with examples.
4. What is mock-heroic, and how does Swift use it in *A Modest Proposal*?
5. Explain the significance of the Restoration reopening of the theatres in 1660.

### Essay questions (challenging)
1. 'Pope's heroic couplet is both a vehicle of wit and a constraint on expression.' Discuss with reference to at least two poems.
2. Analyse the use of irony in Swift's *A Modest Proposal*. How does the satirist use the gap between style and content to create meaning?
3. Compare Restoration comedy of manners with the satire of Swift. How do these modes reflect different attitudes to society?

### Objective / passage-based (moderate to challenging)
1. Which of the following is a feature of the heroic couplet? (a) unrhymed iambic pentameter (b) closed rhyming pairs of iambic pentameter (c) ten syllables with no fixed stress (d) free verse with occasional rhyme.
2. In *A Modest Proposal*, Swift suggests that Irish parents should sell their children as food. This is an example of (a) straightforward advice (b) Juvenalian satire and mock-heroic (c) a realistic economic proposal (d) a Restoration comedy.
3. Passage analysis: Read a couplet from Pope's *Essay on Criticism* and identify the rhyme scheme and the argument of the couplet.
4. Multiple choice: The three unities derive from (a) Shakespeare (b) Aristotle (c) Pope (d) Swift.
