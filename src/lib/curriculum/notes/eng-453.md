## Unit overview

Language and National Development (Kenya) examines the role of English and Kiswahili in Kenyan education, governance, media, and the economy, and the challenges of language planning in a multilingual society. The unit explores how language choices shape national identity, access to opportunity, and social inequality. Students learn to evaluate the functions of English and Kiswahili in Kenyan society, understand the policy framework for mother-tongue-based multilingual education, and assess language as a factor in national development and equity.

The unit grounds its analysis in Kenya's specific linguistic landscape — approximately 70 languages belonging to the Niger-Congo, Nilotic, and Cushitic families, with English and Kiswahili as official languages under the 2010 Constitution. It examines the tensions between globalising forces (the demand for English in higher education and the labour market) and the imperatives of cultural preservation and linguistic human rights. The unit also introduces key concepts from applied linguistics — multilingualism policy, medium of instruction, and linguistic human rights — that are relevant to teaching and curriculum design.

By the end of the unit, students should be able to evaluate the functions of English and Kiswahili in Kenyan society, explain the policy of mother-tongue-based multilingual education and its challenges, and assess language as a factor in national development and inequality. The unit also surfaces the relevance of English for Specific Purposes (ESP) in a Kenyan context, where English is used for specific domains (academic, professional, legal) and where understanding domain-specific language use is essential for effective communication.

## Learning outcomes

- Evaluate the functions of English and Kiswahili in Kenyan society.
- Explain the policy of mother-tongue-based multilingual education (MTB-MLE) and its challenges.
- Assess language as a factor in national development and inequality.
- Describe the ECDE framework and its relationship to language policy in Kenya.
- Explain the concept of linguistic human rights and its relevance to Kenyan education.
- Analyse the medium of instruction debate in the context of Kenyan multilingualism.

## Detailed notes

### Languages in Kenyan society

Kenya is a multilingual nation with approximately 70 languages spoken across its regions. These languages belong to the Niger-Congo, Nilotic, and Cushitic families, with Bantu languages dominant in the central and western regions and Nilotic and Cushitic languages in the Rift Valley and eastern lowlands. **English** and **Kiswahili** are the two official languages enshrined in the 2010 Constitution. English serves as the language of higher education, government, business, and the formal media. Kiswahili is the medium of early primary education (grades 1–3) under the MTB-MLE policy and serves as a unifying lingua franca across ethnic groups. Mother tongues — the languages of individual ethnic communities — serve home, community, and cultural identity.

This three-tier system creates both access and exclusion. Those who enter school with strong competence in English or Kiswahili have an advantage; those whose mother tongue is neither English nor Kiswahili may struggle with the transition to English-medium instruction in upper primary and secondary school. The policy aim is to use the mother tongue as a bridge to English, but implementation gaps — shortage of materials, teacher proficiency, and the pull of English for social mobility — undermine this goal.

### MTB-MLE and ECDE

**Mother-Tongue-Based Multilingual Education (MTB-MLE)** is an approach in which children begin schooling in their mother tongue and gradually transition to additional languages, including Kiswahili and English. The Kenyan Language in Education Policy mandates that instruction in the early grades (1–3) be in the mother tongue, with Kiswahili introduced as a subject and medium, and English introduced progressively. **Early Childhood Education (ECDE)** — covering children aged 0–5 — is the foundation of this system. ECDE programmes in Kenya are intended to be delivered in the mother tongue, but in practice many urban and private ECDE centres use English, reflecting parental demand for early English exposure.

The challenges of MTB-MLE implementation in Kenya are significant. There is a shortage of written materials in many mother tongues, teacher training programmes do not always equip teachers for multilingual classroom practice, and the prestige of English creates pressure to 'skip ahead' to English-medium instruction before learners have solid foundations. Research consistently shows that literacy in a known language boosts learning outcomes, and that denying children instruction in a language they understand entrenches inequality.

### Medium of instruction

The **medium of instruction** — the language used to teach academic content — is one of the most contested issues in Kenyan language policy. The 2010 Constitution and the Language in Education Policy affirm the mother tongue as the medium for early grades, but in practice English dominates from upper primary onwards. The debate centres on a tension between two imperatives: (1) the cognitive benefits of instruction in a familiar language, which supports deeper understanding and higher-order thinking, and (2) the economic and social advantages of English proficiency, which opens access to higher education and employment. The medium of instruction question is not merely linguistic but deeply political, touching on who has access to power and opportunity in Kenyan society.

### Linguistic human rights

**Linguistic human rights** are the rights of individuals and communities to use, develop, and preserve their languages. These rights are recognised in international frameworks such as the Universal Declaration of Linguistic Rights and UNESCO's Language Vitality and Endangerment framework. In the Kenyan context, linguistic human rights include the right of children to be educated in a language they understand, the right of communities to maintain and develop their mother tongues, and the right to access public services in a language one speaks. Language policy in Kenya has implications for linguistic human rights: when mother tongues are marginalised in education and public life, the communities that speak them are effectively denied a dimension of their cultural and political rights.

### Multilingualism policy

Kenya's **multilingualism policy** operates at multiple levels: the constitutional provision for English and Kiswahili as official languages, the Language in Education Policy (MTB-MLE), and the practical realities of a society where most Kenyans are multilingual. The policy framework aims to balance the promotion of national languages (Kiswahili and English) with the recognition of ethnic languages as carriers of cultural identity. However, policy and practice often diverge: the policy supports mother-tongue instruction, but the reality of teacher shortages, material scarcity, and the prestige of English means that English increasingly dominates from the earliest grades.

### ESP connections

**English for Specific Purposes (ESP)** is relevant to this unit because English in Kenya functions as a language for specific domains — academic, legal, medical, business — each with its own registers, conventions, and communicative demands. Understanding ESP is essential for teachers who must prepare students for these domains, and for policymakers who must decide how much English proficiency is needed for different levels of participation in Kenyan society.

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

### Short answer

1. How many languages are estimated to be spoken in Kenya, and to which language families do they belong?
2. What is MTB-MLE, and what is the Kenyan policy on mother-tongue instruction in the early grades?
3. Define ECDE and explain its relationship to language policy in Kenya.
4. What is the 'medium of instruction' debate, and what are the two main imperatives it involves?
5. Explain the concept of linguistic human rights with reference to Kenyan education.
6. How does the three-tier language system in Kenya create both access and exclusion?
7. What are the main challenges of implementing MTB-MLE in Kenyan schools?

### Essay questions

8. 'The medium of instruction is not just a linguistic question but a political one.' Discuss with reference to Kenyan education policy.
9. Evaluate the strengths and weaknesses of MTB-MLE as a language policy for Kenya.
10. How does the concept of linguistic human rights illuminate the tensions in Kenyan language policy?
11. 'English proficiency is essential for social mobility in Kenya, but the dominance of English marginalises mother tongues.' Discuss.

### Objective / passage-based

12. (Difficulty: Easy) According to the Kenyan Constitution, how many official languages are there? (A) One (B) Two (C) Three (D) Four
13. (Difficulty: Medium) Read the following policy statement and identify which principle of MTB-MLE it reflects: 'Learners in Grade 1 should be instructed in their mother tongue before transitioning to Kiswahili and English.'
14. (Difficulty: Hard) Given a case study of a Kenyan school that has abandoned mother-tongue instruction in favour of early English-medium teaching, evaluate the decision in terms of linguistic human rights and educational outcomes.
