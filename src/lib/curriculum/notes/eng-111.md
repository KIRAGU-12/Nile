## Unit overview

An Introduction to Literature equips first-year students with the conceptual vocabulary and analytical methods needed for the critical study of literary texts. The unit traces how imaginative writing in English, African and oral traditions is organised into genres — prose fiction, poetry, drama and oral literature — each governed by formal conventions that shape meaning. For Kenyan students, the unit is especially significant because it validates oral literature as a sophisticated artistic mode rather than a primitive precursor, linking classroom study to living traditions of Gĩkũyũ folktales, Kamba *maua* (riddles) and Kalenjin *bĩrĩku* praise poetry. It also establishes the close-reading method that underpins every subsequent literary unit in the degree.

By grounding literary study in the concrete features of language — diction, imagery, symbol, tone, irony — the unit moves students beyond summary towards interpretation. The skills developed here are directly transferable to the set texts encountered in later units, from Achebe's *Things Fall Apart* to Ngũgĩ's novels. For future teachers of English and literature in Kenyan secondary schools, the unit provides the analytical confidence to lead students through close textual engagement, a core requirement of the national curriculum.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this unit, students should be able to:
- Identify the major literary genres and explain the formal conventions that distinguish each.
- Analyse key elements of literary language, including imagery, symbol, tone, irony and diction, in context.
- Apply close-reading skills to produce interpretive claims grounded in textual evidence.
- Distinguish between literal and figurative language, and between denotation and connotation.
- Evaluate the relationship between oral and written literary traditions in Kenya.
- Construct coherent literary essays with arguable theses, structured paragraphs and proper referencing.

## Detailed notes

### Literary genres and their formal features

Literature is writing that foregrounds language as an artistic and expressive medium rather than a purely functional one. The major genres are distinguished by form and convention. **Prose fiction** is organised in sentences and paragraphs; its material is narrative — a sequence of events developed through plot, character, setting, point of view and technique. The short story typically focuses on a single incident or mood, while the novel sustains a larger, more complex design. **Poetry** is written in verse, arranged in lines and stanzas, and relies on rhythm, sound devices (alliteration, assonance, consonance, rhyme) and compression of meaning. **Drama** is literature in dialogue intended for performance, structured into acts and scenes and realised through speech, stage directions and action. **Oral literature** — folktales, myths, legends, proverbs, riddles, praise poetry and epic — is transmitted by word of mouth; in Kenya it remains a living tradition among Gĩkũyũ, Luo, Kalenjin and other communities, preserving cultural values and worldviews.

### Elements of literary language

Critical reading requires attention to how meaning is made through language. **Diction** is the author's choice of words and registers; **connotation** is a word's associative meaning beyond its dictionary (**denotation**). **Imagery** is language that appeals to the senses. A **symbol** stands for something beyond itself; a **motif** recurs across a text or genre. **Irony** is a discrepancy between expectation and reality — verbal, situational or dramatic. **Tone** is the author's attitude; **mood** is the atmosphere created for the reader. Figurative language includes metaphor, simile, personification, hyperbole, metonymy and synecdoche.

### Close reading and textual analysis

Close reading means reading a text slowly and attentively, noticing how every word and formal choice contributes to meaning. Begin by identifying the surface (literal) meaning, then consider how language and structure produce that meaning and what else they may be doing. A strong analytical paragraph centres on a single point (topic sentence), supports it with specific evidence (quotations and references to the text), and explains how that evidence supports the point. This method is the foundation of all literary essay writing.

**Key terms:** plot · character (flat/round, static/dynamic) · setting · point of view (first/second/third person; limited/omniscient) · theme · tone · mood · irony · symbol · motif · imagery · diction · connotation/denotation · metaphor · simile · personification · genre · stanza · line · rhyme/metre · alliteration/assonance/consonance

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

**Short answer**
1. Define the term "oral literature" and give two examples from a Kenyan community you know. (Easy)
2. Distinguish between connotation and denotation, using the word "home" as an example. (Medium)
3. How does free indirect discourse differ from third-person omniscient narration? Illustrate with a brief example from any short story you have read. (Medium)

**Essay questions**
1. "Oral literature is not a primitive precursor to written literature; it is a sophisticated mode of cultural preservation." Discuss with reference to Kenyan traditions. (Hard)
2. Analyse how imagery, tone and irony work together in one poem or short story studied in class. (Medium)
3. Compare the formal features of poetry and prose fiction, showing how each genre shapes meaning differently. (Medium)

**Objective / passage-based**
1. Read the following extract from Grace Ogot's "The Promised Gift" and answer the questions that follow. (Hard)
   a. Identify the narrative point of view and comment on its effect. (2 marks)
   b. Find two examples of imagery and explain their significance. (4 marks)
   c. How does the author's diction reveal the social status of the characters? (4 marks)
