# ENG 313 — Black Diaspora Literature (Unit Study Guide)

## Unit overview

The transatlantic slave trade (c. 1525–1866) forcibly removed an estimated twelve million Africans from their homelands, creating a global **African diaspora** whose literature gives voice to loss, survival, memory and re-invention. This Unit studies how Black writers across the Americas, the Caribbean and Britain have written back to slavery, colonialism and racism, using **double consciousness** (W.E.B. Du Bois), **rememory** (Toni Morrison) and **nation language** (Kamau Brathwaite) to reclaim identity and history. Kenyan students meet these texts through the lens of postcolonial and Pan-African thought, and in conversation with their own multilingual, multi-ethnic society.

## Learning outcomes

- Trace the historical origins of Black diasporic literature and its key concepts.
- Analyse representations of slavery, freedom, migration and identity across genres.
- Compare how different writers use orality, vernacular and memory as literary resources.
- Interpret the role of language (standard English, Creole, "nation language") in diasporic identity.
- Evaluate contemporary diasporic writing on belonging, hybridity and return.

## Detailed notes

### Origins: the Middle Passage and slave narratives

The **Middle Passage** — the brutal sea journey in slave ships — is the foundational trauma of diasporic literature. **Slave narratives** are its first literary form: autobiographical testimonies that combine Christian conversion rhetoric and Enlightenment ideals with harrowing witness. Frederick Douglass's *Narrative of the Life of a Frederick Douglass, an American Slave* (1845) uses his literacy as both literal skill and metaphor for freedom; Harriet Jacobs's *Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl* (1861) exposes the particular oppression of enslaved women. These texts insist: "I was indeed, indeed present." They established the diasporic strategy of **bearing witness** that later writers would transform into art.

### Double consciousness and the Black intellectual tradition

W.E.B. Du Bois coined **double consciousness** in *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903): the sense of "always looking at one's self through the eyes of others … this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others." The **Talented Tenth**, Du Bois's call for an educated Black leadership, influenced generations. The **Harlem Renaissance** (1920s) gave this consciousness artistic form: **Langston Hughes**' jazz- and blues-infused verse (e.g., "The Weary Blues"), **Zora Neale Hurston**'s use of Black vernacular in *Their Eyes Were Watching God* (1937), and **Claude McKay**'s defiant sonnet "If We Must Die." This was literature asserting a modern, urban, Black aesthetic.

### Language, nation language and the written word

Diasporic writers reclaimed African-rooted culture and language. **Négritude** (Aimé Césaire, Léopold Sédar Senghor) asserted Black cultural values against French colonial denigration — Césaire's *Cahier d'un retour au pays natal* (1939) is a founding text. **Derek Walcott** (Nobel 1992) wrote in standard English but celebrated the Caribbean landscape; his epic *Omeros* (1990) re-tells the *Odyssey* on a Caribbean beach, fusing European form and Caribbean substance. **Kamau Brathwaite** coined **nation language** for the Creole English that carries Caribbean poetic voice ("the boughel is a bird that flies by night" — note the Yoruba-derived orthography), deliberately moving away from the colonial standard. **Benjamin Zephaniah** (British Black) used Jamaican Creole rhythms and police-beat imagery to fuse reggae and poetry.

### Memory, trauma and rememory

Toni Morrison's *Beloved* (1987) — centred on Sethe, an escaped enslaved woman haunted by the daughter she killed to save from slavery — introduces **rememory**: the past is not gone, it is actively remembered and re-membered in the body and the house. Morrison, Nobel laureate (1993), made the unspeakable of slavery speakable in lyrical, non-linear prose. **Contemporary diaspora**: Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie's *Americanah* (2013) and *Half of a Yellow Sun*; Taiye Selasi's *Ghana* (and *The Book of Not*); Dinaw Mengestu's *The Beautiful Things That Heavenly...* explore migration, code-switching and the "ache of a home not quite home."

### Themes across the tradition

- **Displacement and return** — the journey narrative and the impossibility/impossibility of return (e.g., Walcott's "I came back").
- **Naming and renaming** — reclaiming identity from the names imposed by slavery and colonialism.
- **Orality and textuality** — call-and-response, signifying, the ring shout, vernacular speech rendered in standard orthography.
- **Hybridity and multiplicity** — identities forged in the "third space" (Bhabha), never singular.
- **Resistance and critique** — from Nat Turner's rebellion to contemporary Black Lives Matter imagery.

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

### Short answer
1. **(Easy)** What does Du Bois mean by "double consciousness"?
2. **(Medium)** Why is the slave narrative a foundational form for Black diasoric literature?
3. **(Medium)** Distinguish **nation language** (Brathwaite) from **Black English Vernacular**. Give an example.
4. **(Hard)** Explain the concept of *rememory* in *Beloved* and how it structures the novel.
5. **(Hard)** What role does music (blues, jazz, reggae) play in diasporic poetry?
6. **(Medium)** How does *Their Eyes Were Watching God* use vernacular? What is the critical significance?

### Essay questions
1. **(Medium)** Compare the treatment of memory and history in Toni Morrison's *Beloved* and Derek Walcott's *Omeros*.
2. **(Hard)** "Négritude and Black Power are not the same thing." Discuss, referring to Césaire, Senghor, Hughes and/or Zephaniah.
3. **(Hard)** How do Black diasporic writers use language to resist colonial English norms? Illustrate with at least two authors.

### Objective / passage-based
1. **(Easy)** *Beloved* was awarded the Nobel Prize. True or false?
2. **(Easy)** The Harlem Renaissance took place chiefly in:
   a) London b) Harlem, New York c) Paris d) Kingston
3. **(Medium)** Read this line from Brathwaite: "the boughel is a bird that flies by night." What does the spelling signal?
   a) A printing error b) Nation language orthography c) Standard English d) Patois without rules
