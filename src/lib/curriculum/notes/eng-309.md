# ENG 309 — African Literature in English (Unit Study Guide)

## Unit overview

African literature in English is the great postcolonial project of the second half of the twentieth century and beyond: writers taking the coloniser's language and, as Chinua Achebe put it, telling "the African story from the inside." This Unit surveys how African writers have negotiated **orality and the written word**, **tradition and modernity**, **colonial encounter and postcolonial nation-building**, and especially **gender, language and identity**. It foregrounds the Anglophone tradition from Achebe through Ngũgĩ, Emecheta, Nwapa, Soyinka and contemporary voices, and connects their concerns to the Kenyan literary and educational context.

## Learning outcomes

- Situate modern African literature in its historical and cultural contexts.
- Analyse how writers adapt oral forms into written English.
- Interpret literary treatments of colonialism, independence, gender and identity.
- Evaluate debates over language (English vs. African languages) in African literature.
- Compare major African writers' thematic and stylistic concerns.

## Detailed notes

### The birth of the African novel in English

Chinua Achebe's *Things Fall Apart* (1958) is conventionally the starting-point of modern African literature in English. It tells the story of **Okonkwo**, a proud Igbo warrior, whose world is overturned by British colonial rule. The novel's power lies in its **refusal of the "single story"** (Achebe's later phrase): it ends with the District Commissioner's dismissive history of Okonkwo — a reminder that the coloniser still frames the colonised. Achebe adapts **Igbo oral conventions** (proverbs, praise, communal voice) into English prose, and his famous essay "The Danger of a Single Story" warns against Western stereotypes of Africa.

### Language, decolonisation and the African imaginative

Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o's *Decolonising the Mind* (1986) argues that **colonial languages imprison the imagination**. *Weep Not, Child* (1964) — Ngũgĩ's first English novel — follows the Nyeru family torn apart by the Mau Mau uprising: land loss, betrayal, violence, and the destruction of community. Ngũgĩ later abandoned English for Gĩkũyũ, writing *Caita Kĩrĩa* and *Matĩga Mũyũ*; he contends African languages carry knowledge English cannot translate. **Orature** — proverbs, praise poetry, folktales, epic — is not a "primitive" form but a sophisticated mode that African writers bring into the novel (e.g., Soyinka's *Aké*, an autobiography rooted in Yoruba mythology).

### Women's writing and feminist voice

African women writers have centred female experience often marginalised in early nationalist, male-authored narratives. **Buchi Emecheta**'s novels examine women's education, polygamy and urban life in Lagos; **Flora Nwapa**'s *Efuru* (1967) is an Igbo woman's assertion of independence and spiritual power. **Tsitsi Dangarembga**'s *Nervous Conditions* (1988) makes schooling a site both of liberation and of patriarchal control — a key text for Kenyan students of English. Contemporary voices — **Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie** (*Half of a Yellow Sun*, *Purple Hibiscus*), **Binyavanga Wainaina** (*One Day I Will Not Be Here*), **Yaa Gyasi** (*Homegoing*) — write with global reach while remaining rooted in African societies.

### Themes: tradition, modernity, postcolonial identity

Across these writers run recurring concerns:
- **The clash of traditions**: custom vs. Christianity, village vs. city, elder vs. youth.
- **Names and identity**: what it means to be called "Ikem" or "Okonkwo" and to name oneself.
- **Gender and the body**: marriage, polygyny, female genital mutilation, education.
- **English as tool and trap**: the language of schooling, power, and artistic freedom.
- **The postcolony**: corruption, military rule, civil war (Biafra, Kenya's own histories), and the promises that faltered.

Kenyan literature in English — from **Ngũgĩ**, **Meja Mwangi** (*Going Down River Road*, *The Cockroach Dance*), **Pauline Kea** and contemporary poets — shares these preoccupations but also tells specifically Kenyan stories of independence, ethnicity and nation.

### Reading and interpreting African literature

Read these texts on their own terms — as literature, not as ethnography. Attend to **oral rhythms**, **proverbial logic**, **code-switching**, and the **political subtext** of every "tradition." Notice how writers manage the tension between the epics of home and the demands of the English novel form.

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

### Short answer
1. **(Easy)** What does Achebe mean by the "single story"?
2. **(Medium)** How does *Weep Not, Child* represent the Mau Mau uprising differently from colonial histories?
3. **(Medium)** Name two African writers who began writing in English but later shifted to an African language.
4. **(Hard)** What is the significance of the title *Things Fall Apart* (its source is Yeats's "Easter, 1916")?
5. **(Hard)** How does *Nervous Conditions* use schooling to explore gender and power?
6. **(Medium)** Define *orature* and give two examples from African literature.

### Essay questions
1. **(Medium)** "African literature in English is, at its core, a literature of cultural translation." Discuss with reference to any THREE writers.
2. **(Hard)** Compare the treatment of women's agency in *So Long a Letter* (Aminatta Ngũgĩ) and *Nervous Conditions*.
3. **(Hard)** How far is the postcolonial African novel a response to, rather than a rejection of, European literary form?

### Objective / passage-based
1. **(Easy)** *Things Fall Apart* was written by:
   a) Wole Soyinka b) Chinua Achebe c) Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o d) Ben Okri
2. **(Medium)** *Efuru* is notable for being one of the first novels written by:
   a) A Nigerian man b) A West African woman in English c) A missionary d) A Kenyan
3. **(Hard)** Read this excerpt from *Weep Not, Child*: "The tree of knowledge was planted in Paradise for black skins as well as white." What rhetorical tradition is being invoked?
   a) Biblical quotation b) Colonial proverb c) European philosophy d) Indigenous praise
