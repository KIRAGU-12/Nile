## Unit overview

Language and Society introduces students to the core concerns of sociolinguistics: how language varies systematically according to social factors such as class, region, gender and age, and how these variations carry social meaning. The Unit moves from abstract theory to the concrete reality of Kenya's multilingual ecology, where an estimated seventy languages coexist with English and Kiswahili in a dynamic, fluid communicative environment. Understanding this diversity is essential for any English and Literature teacher working in a Kenyan classroom, where students bring a rich repertoire of linguistic resources that shape how they read, write and speak English.

The Unit also addresses the practical implications of language variation for education. Code-switching — the alternation between languages within a conversation or sentence — is the norm in Kenyan daily life, not an exception to be corrected. Diglossia, in which English occupies the high (formal) register and mother-tongues the low (intimate) register, structures the Kenyan educational system in ways that both empower and constrain learners. Language planning — the deliberate choices made by institutions and governments about which languages to use in schooling, media and administration — is examined through the lens of Kenya's own debates over Mother Tongue-Based Multilingual Education (MTB-MLE) and the role of Kiswahili alongside English.

By the end of this Unit, students should be able to analyse linguistic variation in authentic data, explain the social meanings of code-switching and diglossia in the Kenyan context, and evaluate the language-planning issues that shape English and Literature teaching in secondary schools. The Unit bridges linguistics and pedagogy, providing the theoretical foundation for the practical teaching methods explored in EDU 201.

## Learning outcomes

- Analyse patterns of linguistic variation and their social meanings using sociolinguistic concepts.
- Account for multilingualism and code-switching in the Kenyan context, including the emergence of Sheng.
- Explain the concept of diglossia and its implications for English-medium education in Kenya.
- Evaluate language-planning issues facing English, Kiswahili and mother-tongues in the Kenyan curriculum.
- Apply knowledge of social variation (class, region, gender, age) to the analysis of spoken and written texts.
- Critically assess the rationale for MTB-MLE and its implementation challenges in Kenyan schools.

## Detailed notes

### Sociolinguistics and linguistic variation

Sociolinguistics is the study of language in its social and regional variation. No two speakers talk alike, and variation is not random: it correlates systematically with social factors. William Labov's variationist analysis demonstrated that linguistic features — pronunciation, grammar, vocabulary — vary along social dimensions and that these variations carry social meaning. In Kenya, this means that Nairobi English differs from Mombasa English, that a Kikuyu speaker from Kiambu may use different forms than a Luo speaker from Kisumu, and that a teenager's English differs from a teacher's in systematic ways. Students should learn to identify and categorise these patterns rather than treating them as errors.

### Code-switching and multilingualism in Kenya

Kenya is home to roughly seventy languages belonging to the Nilo-Saharan, Niger-Congo, Cushitic and Nilotic families. English and Kiswahili are the official languages, but over sixty other languages are used daily in homes and communities. Most Kenyans are multilingual, routinely speaking an ethnic language, Kiswahili and English. Code-switching — alternating between languages within a conversation or sentence — is the norm, not the exception. For example, a Kenyan speaker might say 'Nimepita kwenye meeting' (I've gone to the meeting), mixing English and Kiswahili seamlessly. This fluidity reflects a communicative competence that should be valued, not pathologised, in the classroom.

### Sheng and urban youth language

Sheng is a urban lingua franca that mixes English, Kiswahili and ethnic-language forms, originating in Nairobi's informal settlements and now spreading across Kenyan urban centres. It is the primary code of many young Kenyans and functions as a marker of identity, solidarity and social belonging. Sheng is not a degraded form of English or Kiswahili but a legitimate, creative linguistic system with its own grammar, lexicon and social conventions. For English teachers, understanding Sheng is important: students who speak Sheng are not deficient in English but are drawing on a rich multilingual repertoire. The challenge is to help them develop register awareness — knowing when to use Sheng, when to use Kiswahili, and when to use standard English — rather than suppressing their home varieties.

### Diglossia and its social implications

Diglossia describes a situation in which two varieties of a language coexist with distinct social functions. In the Kenyan context, English functions as the High (H) variety — used in formal education, government, media and administration — while mother-tongues and Kiswahili function as the Low (L) variety — used in domestic, informal and community settings. The boundary between H and L is constantly negotiated, and individuals move between varieties depending on context. Diglossia has significant implications for education: students whose home language is not English must acquire the H variety as a second (or third) language, and the classroom becomes the primary site for this acquisition. This creates both opportunities and challenges for English and Literature teaching.

### Language planning and MTB-MLE in Kenya

Language planning refers to deliberate choices made by institutions and governments about which languages to use in education, media and public life. Kenya's 2010 Constitution recognises the role of Kiswahili as a national language and the diversity of other languages. The introduction of MTB-MLE (Mother Tongue-Based Multilingual Education) in Kenyan primary schools represents a significant policy shift: children are taught in their mother-tongue in the early years before transitioning to English and Kiswahili. The rationale is that literacy in the mother tongue provides a foundation for acquiring additional languages. However, implementation faces challenges: teacher availability in local languages, the lack of written standards for many languages, and the prestige attached to English as the language of economic mobility. For English teachers, MTB-MLE raises the question of how to build on students' existing linguistic resources rather than treating them as deficits.

### Gender, age and class variation

Linguistic variation also correlates with gender, age and social class. Women and men may use different vocabulary, address forms and politeness strategies; research in Kenya has shown that women tend to use more standard forms in formal settings, while men may use more vernacular features. Age variation reflects both generational change (younger speakers innovating faster) and the life-course (language use shifting as individuals move between social roles). Class variation is perhaps the most politically sensitive: the prestige variety associated with educated, urban speakers is often treated as the standard, while rural and working-class varieties are stigmatised. In the Kenyan classroom, these patterns intersect with ethnicity and region, creating a complex landscape of variation that teachers must navigate with sensitivity and linguistic awareness.

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

### Short answer (moderate)
1. Define code-switching and give two examples of how it operates in Kenyan daily life.
2. What is Sheng, and why is it significant for English language teaching in Kenya?
3. Explain the concept of diglossia and identify the H and L varieties in the Kenyan context.
4. What is MTB-MLE, and what is its rationale in Kenyan education policy?
5. How does Labov's variationist approach differ from prescriptive approaches to language?

### Essay questions (challenging)
1. 'Code-switching is a resource, not a problem.' Discuss this claim with reference to Kenyan classrooms.
2. Analyse the social implications of diglossia for English-medium education in Kenya. What challenges does it pose for literature teaching?
3. Compare the linguistic features of Sheng with those of a traditional Kenyan language variety. What does this comparison reveal about language change and identity?

### Objective / passage-based (moderate to challenging)
1. In the following passage, identify two instances of code-switching: 'I went to the market and bought matunda, then I met my friend and we talked about the exam.'
2. Which of the following best describes diglossia? (a) speakers of two languages mixing freely (b) two varieties of the same language serving different social functions (c) a language dying out (d) a pidgin becoming a creole. Justify your answer.
3. Passage analysis: Read a short dialogue in which a Kenyan student switches between English and Kiswahili. What social meanings does the switching create?
4. Multiple choice: MTB-MLE stands for (a) Mother Tongue-Based Multilingual Education (b) Multilingual Teaching by Alternative Methods (c) Mother Tongue Bridge to Multilingual Education (d) Multilingual Teaching Based on Mother Languages.
