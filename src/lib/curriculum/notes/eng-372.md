# ENG 372 — English Poetry: Romantic to Modern (Exam Study Guide)

## Unit overview

This Unit surveys English‑language poetry from the late eighteenth century through the early twentieth, mapping the shift from the Romantic celebration of feeling and nature to the Modernist fragmentation and disillusionment of the post‑war era. It begins with the “big six” Romantic poets — **William Wordsworth**, **Samuel Taylor Coleridge**, **Lord Byron**, **Percy Bysshe Shelley**, **John Keats** and **William Hazlitt** — who, in Lyrical Ballads (1798), declared poetry the “spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings.” The Romantic “self” is expansive, imaginative, and in dialogue with nature; Byron’s “Byronic hero” is charismatic yet rebellious; Keats’s “negative capability” tolerates uncertainty. Victorian poetry grows more narrative and philosophical — Tennyson grapples with faith after Darwin, Browning explores interior psychology through the dramatic monologue, Arnold mourns the retreat of belief.

The second half of the Unit moves to **Modernism**. T.S. Eliot’s *The Waste Land* (1922) — “I will show you fear in a handful of dust” — collages voices, myths and allusions to portray a spiritually sterile age; Ezra Pound’s maxim “Make it new” drives imagist precision; W.B. Yeats evolves from Celtic‑Revival lyricist to modernist of political and mystical verse. The trauma of the First World War produced **war poetry** — Siegfried Sassoon and Wilfred Owen’s graphic anti‑heroic testimony — which broke decisively with Victorian idealisation. The Unit closes on the threshold of confessional and post‑modern verse (Auden, the Rossettis, Hopkins’s “sprung rhythm”), showing how each generation redefined the poem’s relation to tradition, the self, and the contemporary world.

## Learning outcomes

- Trace the stylistic and thematic shifts from Romantic lyric to Modernist fragmentation.
- Analyse how poetry engages with nature, the self, history and modernity.
- Interpret the break with tradition in Modernist and post‑Romantic verse.
- Evaluate the role of form (imagism, dramatic monologue, sprung rhythm) in meaning‑making.
- Compare how individual poets respond to the same historical pressures.
- Demonstrate close reading of poetic diction, imagery, rhythm and structure.

## Detailed notes

### Late Romanticism: Byron, Keats, Shelley

The Romantic poets revalued the self, the imagination and nature. **Lord Byron** created the **Byronic hero** — a charismatic, brooding rebel alienated from society (seen in *Childe Harold’s Pilgrimage*). **Percy Bysshe Shelley** wrote radical lyric poetry (“Ozymandias,” “To a Skylark”) that blends political dissent with visionary imagination. **John Keats** introduced **“negative capability”** — the capacity to exist in uncertainties, mysteries, doubts — and explored the **“urn of ancient lore”** in his “Odes,” where beauty and truth are contemplated without logical resolution. Keats’s notion of “**subjective truth**” — what the mind feels to be true — is vital for understanding Romantic epistemology. The Romantic emphasis on **spontaneity** (“licks its own forge”) contrasts sharply with later Victorian formality.

### Victorian poetry: faith, doubt and the dramatic monologue

Victorian poets grappled with science and religion. **Alfred, Lord Tennyson’s** *In Memoriam* registers the crisis of Darwinism (the poem’s “Nature, red in tooth and claw”) but ultimately affirms faith. **Robert Browning** revolutionised lyric poetry through the **dramatic monologue**: a speaker addresses a silent listener and inadvertently reveals his psychology and moral blindness (“My Last Ductor,” “Porphyria’s Lover”). **Matthew Arnold’s** “Dover Beach” replaces religious consolation with melancholic realism: the “Sea of Faith” has retreated, leaving “the grim Head but the crumble of things.” Arnold’s detached, “scholar‑gentleman” tone influences later British poetry. The Pre‑Raphaelites — **Dante Gabriel Rossetti**, **Christina and William Michael Rossetti**, **Algernon Swinburne**, **William Morris** — revived medieval colour, sensuous imagery and intense personal emotion; Christina Rossetti’s *Goblin Market* stages female desire and sisterhood in a symbolist idiom.

### Gerard Manley Hopkins and sprung rhythm

Hopkins (1844–1889), unpublished in his lifetime, broke with Victorian prosody. He coined **“inscape”** — the unique individual design that makes each thing itself — and **“instress”** — the experience of that design. His **“sprung rhythm”** (stressed‑syllable count rather than syllable count) and **“sonic bite”** (tremor, stress, alliteration) make form embody meaning: “The wind‑puff‑bonnet of the breezy down‑y” enacts the very breath it describes. Hopkins’s compressed, neologistic style points forwards to Modernism.

### Modernism: Eliot, Pound, Yeats and fragmentation

Modernist poets rejected the expressive lyric “I” and the linear story. **T.S. Eliot’s** *The Waste Land* fragments voices — a pub‑conversation in London, a Brahminical blessing, a German nympholept — connected by mythic scaffolding (the Fisher King). The poem’s **collage technique**, its **allusive density**, and its invocation of spiritual sterility (“I will show you fear…”) make it the period’s defining text. **Ezra Pound** championed **imagism** — direct treatment of the “thing,” no word added — and edited *The Waste Land* into final shape. **W.B. Yeats** evolved from Celtic‑Revival lyricism (“The Lake Isle of Innisfree”) to modernist political mysticism (“Easter, 1916,” “The Second Coming”). Modernist fragmentation mirrors the fractured self and the collapse of shared certainties.

### War poetry: Sassoon, Owen and anti‑heroic witness

The First World War produced a radical rupture in tone. **Siegfried Sassoon** (“Suicide in the Ward”) and **Wilfred Owen** (“Dulce et Decorum Est”) rejected patriotic glorification, instead offering **anti‑heroic witness**: gas attack, shell shock, young death. Owen’s **pararhyme** (near‑rhymes: “men / meant”) enacts disorientation; his “**Dulce**” directly condemns the “old Lie: Dulce et decorum est / Pro patria mori.” These poets use **catalogue, repetition and stark imagery** to make readers witness industrial killing. Kenyan readers can compare Owen’s trench testimony with contemporary conflict poetry such as that of **Okwiri Oduor** or **Sitawa Namwal** — the same anti‑heroic impulse.

### Aftermath: Auden, the Confessional and Larkin

**W.H. Auden** (“September 1, 1939,” “In Memory of W.B. Yeats”) mixed public politics with private psychology, writing in a demotic idiom accessible to mass audiences. The **Confessional poets** — **Sylvia Plath**, **Anne Sexton** — turned inward with shocking intimacy (“Daddy,” “Tulips”). **Philip Larkin** (“Church Going,” “Annus Mirabilis”) offered dry, skeptical meditations on ordinary disappointment. Each represents a further step away from Romantic transcendence towards a poetry that finds its material in the immediately, often uncomfortably contemporary.

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

### Short answer
1. **(Easy)** Define “negative capability.”
2. **(Medium)** Explain the significance of the “Sea of Faith” in Arnold’s “Dover Beach.”
3. **(Medium)** What is “sprung rhythm” and why does Hopkins use it?
4. **(Hard)** Describe two modernist techniques in *The Waste Land*.

### Essay questions
1. **(Medium)** “Romantic poetry redefined the relationship between the self and nature.” Discuss with reference to at least two poets.
2. **(Hard)** Compare Browning’s and Arnold’s responses to the Victorian crisis of faith.
3. **(Hard)** Assess the modernist break with Romantic lyric — is it a clean break or continuous?

### Objective / passage-based
1. **(Easy)** Which poet wrote “Make it new”?
   - A) T.S. Eliot B) Ezra Pound C) W.B. Yeats D) W.H. Auden
2. **(Medium)** “Beauty is truth, truth beauty — that is all / Ye know on earth, and all ye need to know.” Identify the speaker/narrator and context.
3. **(Hard)** Extract: “I have heard / The key / Turn in the door once and turn once only.” What does this image do for the poem’s tone?
