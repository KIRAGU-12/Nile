## Unit overview

Geoffrey Chaucer (c. 1340s–1400) is the towering figure of late medieval English literature, often called the 'father of English literature' for his decisive role in establishing English as a vehicle for sophisticated literary art at a time when French and Latin dominated the courts of Europe. His masterpiece, *The Canterbury Tales*, is a frame narrative: thirty pilgrims from different social estates gather at the Tabard Inn in London and agree to tell stories on the road to Canterbury Cathedral, competing for a free meal at the journey's end. The work offers an unparalleled panorama of late-medieval English society — from the noble Knight to the bawdy Miller, the worldly Friar to the assertive Wife of Bath — and remains a cornerstone of the English literary canon.

Chaucer's narrative art is remarkable for its psychological depth, its genre awareness and its linguistic experimentation. He wrote in Middle English, blending the London dialect with colloquial and elevated registers, and his use of iambic pentameter in rhymed couplets (the heroic couplet) would shape English poetry for centuries. The Tales range across genres — chivalric romance, fabliau, sermon, confession, allegory — and each tale both performs and critiques its genre, often through the ironic contrast between the teller and the tale. Chaucer's character-portraits, introduced in the General Prologue, balance ideal and reality, revealing the gap between social appearance and individual conduct.

This Unit builds on the foundations laid in ENG 215 (Old English) by tracing the development of English as a literary language from the Anglo-Saxon period through the Middle English era. Students will learn to analyse frame narratives, to distinguish and evaluate generic conventions, and to appreciate Chaucer's linguistic and metrical innovations. The Unit also prepares students for the study of Renaissance literature in ENG 231 by tracing the continuity of literary tradition from the medieval to the early modern period.

## Learning outcomes

- Situate *The Canterbury Tales* within late-medieval English culture and the tradition of the frame narrative.
- Analyse Chaucer's use of character-portraiture, irony and genre variety in the Tales.
- Explain the significance of iambic pentameter, rhymed couplets and linguistic variation in Middle English.
- Evaluate the social commentary embedded in Chaucer's portrayal of the pilgrimage party.
- Compare the generic conventions of at least two tale-types represented in the collection.
- Assess Chaucer's contribution to the development of English as a literary language.

## Detailed notes

### The frame narrative and the pilgrimage frame

A frame narrative is a story within which other stories are embedded; the outer frame provides context, commentary and a organising structure. In *The Canterbury Tales*, the outer frame is the pilgrimage to the shrine of Thomas Becket at Canterbury Cathedral. Chaucer's narrator meets thirty pilgrims at the Tabard Inn in Southwark and joins the company, with each pilgrim agreeing to tell two tales on the way and two on the return. The frame serves multiple functions: it provides a reason for the gathering, it establishes a competitive structure (the Host Harry Bailey judges the tales), and it creates a microcosm of medieval society in which the teller's identity shapes the tale's meaning. The General Prologue, which introduces each pilgrim, is itself a masterclass in character-portraiture and social observation.

### Genre variety in the Tales

The Tales range across a remarkable variety of genres, each with its own conventions and expectations. The Knight's Tale is a chivalric romance, elevated in tone and concerned with courtly love and the rivalry between two knights for the same woman. The Miller's Tale is a fabliau — a bawdy, comic story that mocks the gullibility of the bourgeois clerk John and the cuckolding of the old carpenter. The Wife of Bath's Prologue is a confessional narrative that asserts female experience and authority over marriage and sexuality, challenging the misogynistic traditions of medieval literature. The Pardoner's Tale is a morality tale, one of the darkest in the collection, built around the maxim 'Radix malorum est cupiditas' (greed is the root of all evil). Chaucer's genius lies in making each tale both conform to and subvert its generic expectations, creating layers of irony that reward close reading.

### Iambic pentameter and Middle English verse

Chaucer wrote in iambic pentameter — ten syllables per line with an unstressed-STRESSED pattern — and used the rhymed couplet (heroic couplet) as his primary verse form. This metre, which would dominate English poetry for centuries, gave Chaucer's verse a natural, speech-like rhythm that distinguished it from the more rigid Old English alliterative line. His language mixes the London dialect with colloquial and elevated registers, showing the flexibility that English gained from contact with French and Latin after the Norman Conquest. Reading Chaucer in the original Middle English reveals a language in transition, with spelling and pronunciation that differ from modern English but remain recognisable. For Kenyan students, this linguistic flexibility mirrors the code-mixing and register-shifting they perform daily between English, Kiswahili and their mother-tongues.

### Character portraiture and irony

Chaucer's General Prologue introduces each pilgrim through a portrait that balances ideal and reality. The Knight is presented in ideal terms — he fought in the Holy Land, is modest and honourable — while others expose the gap between appearance and conduct: the Prioress is fussy and affected, the Friar is worldly and greedy, the Pardoner preaches against greed while selling fake relics. This pattern of contrast and irony is Chaucer's central critical technique. The portraits are not merely satirical; they are psychologically nuanced, revealing the complexity of human motivation. Students should learn to read the General Prologue as both a social document and a work of art, noting how Chaucer's narrator — often assumed to be Chaucer himself — uses irony to comment on the pilgrims' stories and on the society they represent.

### Chaucer's linguistic legacy

Chaucer's decision to write in English, at a time when French and Latin were the prestige literary languages, was a landmark in the history of English literature. His London-based dialect became the basis for standard English, and his metrical innovations — iambic pentameter in the heroic couplet — became the dominant verse form for centuries. Chaucer also demonstrated that English could handle the full range of literary genres, from the elevated (the Knight's Tale) to the bawdy (the Miller's Tale), from the philosophical (the Clerk's Tale) to the personal (the Wife of Bath's Prologue). This versatility helped establish English as a legitimate literary language and paved the way for the Renaissance writers — Sidney, Shakespeare, Spenser — who would inherit and transform Chaucer's legacy.

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

### Short answer (moderate)
1. What is a frame narrative, and how does the pilgrimage structure function in *The Canterbury Tales*?
2. Define the fabliau and give an example from Chaucer. What does the genre reveal about medieval social attitudes?
3. Explain how Chaucer uses iambic pentameter in the heroic couplet.
4. What is the significance of the General Prologue as a social document?
5. How does the Wife of Bath challenge medieval literary conventions about women?

### Essay questions (challenging)
1. 'Chaucer's irony is the key to understanding his social commentary.' Discuss with reference to at least two pilgrims and their tales.
2. Compare the Knight's Tale and the Miller's Tale as examples of how Chaucer uses genre to explore social class and literary taste.
3. How does Chaucer's use of the frame narrative create meaning that a simple collection of tales could not achieve?

### Objective / passage-based (moderate to challenging)
1. In the General Prologue, the Knight is described as having fought in 'the worthy fields of Flanders.' What does this detail reveal about Chaucer's method of characterisation?
2. Which of the following best describes the genre of the Miller's Tale? (a) chivalric romance (b) fabliau (c) allegory (d) sermon. Justify your answer.
3. Passage analysis: Read the opening lines of the General Prologue ('Whan that Aprille with his shoures soote...') and identify two features of Chaucer's Middle English that differ from modern English.
4. Multiple choice: The Host Harry Bailey's role in the frame narrative is primarily to (a) tell the best tale (b) judge the tales and keep order (c) fund the pilgrimage (d) translate the tales into French.
