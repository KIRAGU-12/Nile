# ENG 302 — The English Novel (19th Century) (Exam Study Guide)

## Unit overview

The English novel emerged in the eighteenth century as a form designed to represent ordinary individual experience, and it reached its mature realism in the nineteenth century. This Unit traces the novel from its satirical origins in **Daniel Defoe’s** *Robinson Crusoe* (1719) — the first English novel often said to be so because of its detailed, first‑person accounting of “real” events — through **Samuel Richardson’s** *Pamela* (a moral epistolary novel) and **Henry Fielding’s** *Tom Jones* (1749), an exuberantly comic epic of English life, to the Victorian giants Charles Dickens, the Brontës, George Eliot and Thomas Hardy. The serial publication of Dickens’s novels in monthly parts shaped narrative structure and pacing: each installment ended on a hook that compelled the subscriber to buy the next number. This serial rhythm influenced plot construction, character presentation and the length of scenes throughout the century.

Central to the Unit is the technical innovation of **free indirect discourse** — the narrator’s report of a character’s thoughts and speech in the character’s own idiom while retaining narrative distance. Jane Austen perfected the device in works such as *Pride and Prejudice* and *Emma*, producing irony through the gap between what characters think and the reader’s fuller knowledge. The Brontës and George Eliot extended this focalisation towards the psychological modernism of the early twentieth century. The Unit also examines how novels responded to — and shaped — social and cultural change: industrialisation, the rise of the middle class, the “woman question,” and, in Hardy, the deterministic pressures of environment and heredity that heralded literary modernism.

## Learning outcomes

- Trace the formal development of the English novel from 1700 to 1900.
- Identify and analyse narrative techniques including free indirect discourse and focalisation.
- Interpret novels as responses to social, economic and cultural transformation.
- Compare comic satire, realist and naturalist modes.
- Evaluate the significance of serial publication for narrative structure.
- Relate the 19th‑century novel to the threshold of modernism.

## Detailed notes

### Origins: from Robinson Crusoe to Tom Jones

**Daniel Defoe**’s *Robinson Crusoe* (1719) is often dated as the first English novel because it presents an apparently authentic, first‑person account of individual survival rendered in realistic detail. Its importance lies less in plot than in **epistolary/vernacular authenticity** — the claim that an ordinary life is worth recording. **Samuel Richardson’s** *Pamela; or, Virtue Rewarded* (1740) turns the epistolary novel to moral instruction, showing the servant‑girl’s virtue tested by seduction; its success proved a market existed for novels of domestic feeling. **Henry Fielding’s** *Tom Jones* (1749) answered Richardson with comic swagger: a Foundling hero roams Georgian England, encountering types from every class, while a knowing narrator comments on the action. Fielding establishes the **comic epic** mode that Dickens inherits.

### Serial publication and narrative architecture

The serial form — publication in monthly, weekly or penny‑part installments — revolutionised the novel. **Charles Dickens** wrote directly into this market: *The Pickwick Papers*, *Oliver Twist*, *Hard Times*, *Bleak House*. Serialisation demanded **cliff‑hanger chapter endings**, a large cast, and a pace that sustained interest across months. **Elizabeth Gaskell’s** *North and South* was published in Household Words, Dickens’s weekly; her plotting reflects serial rhythms. The serial also democratised reading: middle‑class families subscribed, and the price of a part made the story affordable to workers. Understanding seriality helps explain why 19th‑century novels can feel long, episodic, and populated with many subplots — each subplot sustains a month’s worth of narrative suspense.

### Free indirect discourse and focalisation

Perhaps the novel’s greatest technical invention, **free indirect discourse** (also called *free indirect style*) lets the narrator report a character’s thoughts in the character’s idiom, vocabulary and emotional register while maintaining third‑person authority. Austen’s famous opening — “It is a truth universally acknowledged…” — is ironic free indirect discourse: the narrator speaks in the register of the well‑to‑do society while the reader detects the satire. George Eliot pushed the technique towards interiority in *Middlemarch*; the Brontës (especially *Jane Eyre*’s “I was a discordant discord”) blurred the boundary between first‑person feeling and omniscient commentary, foreshadowing modernist stream of consciousness.

### Realism, sensationalism and the social novel

**Realism** aims at a truthful representation of ordinary life, with detailed settings, psychologically motivated characters and causal plotting. **Social problem novels** (Dickens’s *Hard Times*, Gaskell’s *Mary Barton*) used realism to expose poverty, education reform and factory conditions. By contrast, **sensation fiction** (Wilkie Collins) employed mystery, forgery and bigamy to explore moral danger within the domestic sphere. Both modes share the assumption that literature can reform society; the difference is in tone — Dickens’s overt indictment versus Collins’s teasing revelation of hidden crimes behind respectable facades.

### Fin de siècle: Hardy and naturalistic determinism

**Thomas Hardy** stands at the threshold of modernism. *Tess of the d’Urbervilles* (1891) and *Jude the Obscure* (1895) subject their protagonists to **naturalistic determinism**: poverty, heredity, and indifferent social forces crush individual aspiration. Hardy’s Wessex is meticulously mapped, yet his gods‑like narrators comment ironically on fate. His treatment of sexuality, marriage and class scandalised contemporary readers and prepared the ground for **modernist fragmentation** — the idea that language and narrative must break if they are to represent a world in which traditional certainties have failed. **Robert Louis Stevenson**, **Rudyard Kipling** and **H. Rider Haggard** supplied the imperial romance that Hardy’s bleak domestic realism balanced against.

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

### Short answer
1. **(Easy)** What is free indirect discourse? Give one example from *Pride and Prejudice*.
2. **(Medium)** How did serial publication influence the structure of Dickens’s novels?
3. **(Medium)** Explain the difference between realism and sensationalism with reference to one set text.
4. **(Hard)** Account for Thomas Hardy’s reputation as a “naturalist” writer.

### Essay questions
1. **(Medium)** Compare the treatment of the individual and society in *Tom Jones* and *Jane Eyre*.
2. **(Hard)** “The 19th‑century English novel documents and critiques social change.” Discuss with reference to at least three texts.
3. **(Hard)** Assess the significance of free indirect discourse for the development of the novel.

### Objective / passage-based
1. **(Easy)** Which author wrote the first English epistolary novel of manners?
   - A) Defoe B) Richardson C) Fielding D) Austen
2. **(Medium)** “My father — I should say, my father and mother.” Identify the narrating voice and comment on what it reveals about focalisation.
3. **(Hard)** Extract: “I am the servant of the pen.” Whose voice is this, and what novelistic technique is being exemplified?
