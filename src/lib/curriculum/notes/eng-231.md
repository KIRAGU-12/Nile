## Unit overview

Renaissance Literature covers the major poetry and drama of the English Renaissance, a period of extraordinary literary and cultural transformation roughly spanning 1500 to 1660. The Unit centres on Shakespeare — his tragedies, comedies, histories and sonnets — but also engages with the broader humanist movement that revalued classical learning and placed human experience at the centre of literary inquiry. The Renaissance was a period of confidence and anxiety in equal measure: the recovery of classical texts, the expansion of the theatre as a public institution, the Protestant Reformation and the Elizabethan settlement all press into the literature, creating works of enduring complexity.

The Unit introduces students to the sonnet tradition — from Petrarch's Italian model to the English (Shakespearean) sonnet — and to the drama of revenge tragedy pioneered by Kyd and developed by Marlowe and Shakespeare. It also addresses the metaphysical poets, whose witty, argumentative style and use of the conceit (an extended, often surprising metaphor) mark a distinct strand of Renaissance verse. The Jacobean city — the urban world of London in the early seventeenth century, with its theatres, taverns, courts and crowded streets — provides the social backdrop for much of the drama and poetry of the period.

Students will learn to analyse Renaissance texts with attention to form (the sonnet sequence, blank verse, the stage conventions of Elizabethan and Jacobean drama), to situate them within their historical and intellectual contexts, and to appreciate the range of genres — from the intimate lyric to the public spectacle of the playhouse — that the period produced. The Unit builds on the medieval foundations of ENG 215 and ENG 216 and prepares students for the neoclassical and Romantic periods in ENG 241 and ENG 242.

## Learning outcomes

- Situate Renaissance writing within humanist culture and Reformation debate.
- Analyse Shakespearean tragedy and comedy in their dramatic and historical contexts.
- Interpret the English sonnet sequence and the Petrarchan influence on Renaissance poetry.
- Explain the conventions of revenge tragedy and Marlovian blank-verse drama.
- Identify and analyse the metaphysical conceit as a poetic device.
- Evaluate the significance of the Jacobean city as a literary and cultural setting.

## Detailed notes

### Humanism and the drama of revenge

Renaissance humanism restored the study of classical Greek and Roman texts and placed humans, reason and classical learning at the centre of inquiry. This confidence — in human capacity, in the value of earthly life, in the power of reason — was later tested by religious scepticism and the Reformation, which fractured the religious unity of Europe and introduced new anxieties about salvation, conscience and authority. Drama moved from the medieval mystery and morality plays to secular forms: the city comedy, the revenge tragedy and the history play. Revenge tragedy, exemplified by Kyd's *The Spanish Tragedy* (c. 1587), dramatised the problem of private justice in a corrupt world: the protagonist seeks vengeance for a wrong that the law cannot or will not redress, and the pursuit of revenge corrupts the avenger. Shakespeare's problem plays and tragedies develop these themes with psychological complexity, exploring guilt, ambition, madness and the structure of power.

### The Petrarchan and Shakespearean sonnet

The sonnet is a fourteen-line poem in iambic pentameter, and the Renaissance saw the sonnet sequence become a major literary form. The Petrarchan (Italian) sonnet divides into an octave (abbaabba) and a sestet (cdcdcd or variant), with a volta or turn between the two halves. Petrarch's model, developed in the *Canzoniere*, explored the poet's unrequited love for Laura and established conventions — the beloved as distant, idealised, unattainable — that influenced English poets for centuries. The English (Shakespearean) sonnet rearranges the rhyme scheme to three quatrains and a couplet (abab cdcd efef gg), allowing a more argumentative, develop-and-resolve structure. Shakespeare's 154 sonnets investigate beauty, mortality, time, desire and the power of poetry itself, often addressing a young man (the Fair Youth) and a dark lady, and often unsettling the conventions of the Petrarchan tradition.

### Shakespeare's tragedies: form and meaning

Shakespeare's great tragedies — *Hamlet*, *Macbeth*, *King Lear*, *Othello* — explore the extremes of human experience: guilt, ambition, madness, jealousy, the breakdown of authority. *Macbeth* (1606) compresses guilt and horror through imagery of blood, darkness and sleepwalking; the witches' prophecy, Lady Macbeth's ambition and Duncan's murder set in motion a spiral of violence that ends in tyranny and defeat. *Hamlet* (c. 1600) is the exemplary revenge tragedy that becomes a meditation on thought, action, mortality and appearance; 'To be, or not to be' frames the debate between life and death, and the play-within-a-play ('The Mousetrap') stages the problem of representation itself. *King Lear* confronts ageing, authority, family and justice in a world where natural order breaks down — the storm on the heath mirrors the king's inner and outer chaos.

### Shakespeare's comedies and histories

Shakespeare's comedies — *A Midsummer Night's Dream*, *Twelfth Night*, *Much Ado About Nothing* — use mistaken identity, word-play, disguise and festive reconciliation to explore love, gender and social order. The comedies often end in marriage, but Shakespeare complicates the resolution: *Twelfth Night* questions the stability of gender identity, *Much Ado About Nothing* exposes the violence beneath social wit, and *A Midsummer Night's Dream* blurs the boundary between the fairy world and the human. The histories — *Henry IV* (Parts 1 and 2), *Richard III* — examine legitimacy, political power and the construction of national identity. Shakespeare's histories are not straightforwardly patriotic; they present the costs of political ambition and the instability of the throne.

### Marlovian tragedy and the revenge tradition

Christopher Marlowe (1564–1593) was the pioneer of English blank-verse drama of tyranny and overreaching. His plays — *Tamburlaine*, *Doctor Faustus*, *The Jew of Malta* — feature protagonists of extraordinary ambition who defy the limits of human power and are destroyed for their hubris. *Doctor Faustus* dramatises Renaissance human ambition and damnation: Faustus sells his soul to Lucifer in exchange for knowledge and worldly power, and the play's tragic energy comes from the tension between Faustus's intellectual hunger and the moral consequences of his choice. Marlowe's blank verse — unrhymed iambic pentameter — became the standard metre for English drama, and his influence on Shakespeare is profound. The revenge tradition that Marlowe helped establish — private vengeance in a world where the law is corrupt or absent — runs through the period's drama and into the Jacobean tragedies of Webster and Tourneur.

### Metaphysical conceit and the Jacobean city

The metaphysical poets — John Donne (1572–1631) foremost among them — developed a style characterised by the metaphysical conceit: an extended, often startling metaphor that draws a surprising connection between two unlike things. Donne's love poetry fuses the physical and the spiritual: in 'The Good-Morrow', the lovers' bed becomes the centre of the universe; in the Holy Sonnets, sin and salvation are argued through legal and geographical metaphors. The Jacobean city — London in the early seventeenth century — provided the setting for much of this poetry and drama: its theatres (the Globe, the Blackfriars), its courts, its taverns and its crowded streets created a world of social mobility, sexual licence, political intrigue and intellectual ferment. The city was both a place of opportunity and a site of moral danger, and this duality pervades the literature of the period.

### Stylistics: Renaissance diction and metre

Stylistics — the linguistic analysis of literary texts — is particularly valuable for Renaissance literature. Renaissance poets and dramatists manipulated diction (the choice of words) to signal class, education and genre: the elevated diction of the court masque differs from the colloquial energy of the city comedy. Blank verse (unrhymed iambic pentameter) became the dominant dramatic metre, and its flexibility — allowing both speech-like rhythms and heightened poetic effects — made it ideal for the theatre. Metaphysical poets used syntax in innovative ways: abrupt openings, paradoxes, enjambment and complex argument structures that mirror the intellectual energy of their verse. Students should learn to read Renaissance texts with attention to both the linguistic surface and the social and intellectual contexts that shape it.

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

### Short answer (moderate)
1. Define the metaphysical conceit and give an example from Donne.
2. Distinguish between the Petrarchan and Shakespearean sonnet forms.
3. What is blank verse, and why did it become the dominant metre for English drama?
4. Explain the concept of revenge tragedy with reference to Kyd and Marlowe.
5. How does Shakespeare complicate the comic resolution in *Twelfth Night* or *Much Ado About Nothing*?

### Essay questions (challenging)
1. 'The revenge tragedy is as much about the corruption of the avenger as about the original crime.' Discuss with reference to *Hamlet* and one other text.
2. Compare the treatment of love in a Petrarchan sonnet sequence with Shakespeare's sonnets. How does Shakespeare adapt and subvert the Petrarchan model?
3. Analyse the role of the Jacobean city as a literary setting in one play or poem from the period.

### Objective / passage-based (moderate to challenging)
1. In the following lines from Marlowe's *Doctor Faustus*, identify the metre: 'Was this the face that launch'd a thousand ships / And burnt the topless towers of Ilium?' (a) trochaic tetrameter (b) iambic pentameter (c) anapestic hexameter (d) free verse.
2. Which of the following best describes a metaphysical conceit? (a) a simple simile (b) an extended, surprising metaphor linking unlike things (c) a rhyme scheme (d) a dramatic monologue.
3. Passage analysis: Read a sonnet by Shakespeare and identify the volta (turn) and explain how it shapes the poem's argument.
4. Multiple choice: The Jacobean city in Renaissance literature is best characterised as (a) a rural idyll (b) a site of moral and social complexity (c) a purely fictional setting (d) a place of political stability.
