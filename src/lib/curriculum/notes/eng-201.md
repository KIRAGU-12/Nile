## Unit overview

Introduction to Literary Criticism provides a historical overview of the Western tradition of interpreting and evaluating literature, from classical antiquity to the early nineteenth century. The Unit examines how writers and thinkers have answered the fundamental questions: What is literature? How should it be read? What is its value? The journey begins with Plato's famous banishment of the poets from his ideal republic in *The Republic*, where he argues that poetry is imitation (mimesis) and morally dangerous because it appeals to the lower part of the soul. Aristotle's *Poetics* offers a counter-argument: tragedy, far from being harmful, purges the emotions of pity and fear through catharsis, and plot — the 'soul of tragedy' — is the most important element of dramatic art.

The Unit then moves through the Renaissance defence of poetry (Sidney's *Defence of Poesy*), the practical criticism of Dryden and Johnson, and the Romantic revolution in critical theory (Wordsworth's *Preface to Lyrical Ballads*). Each period develops its own assumptions about the nature and value of literature, and students learn to identify these assumptions and to evaluate them critically. The Unit also introduces modern critical theory — the shift toward historical, psychological and ideological approaches to literature — and prepares students for the more advanced literary theory they will encounter in later years. The Unit builds on the literary content of ENG 215–242 and provides the theoretical framework for interpreting the texts studied across the programme.

## Learning outcomes

- Describe classical and medieval theories of poetry and drama, including Plato's mimesis and Aristotle's catharsis.
- Compare Renaissance defences of poetry with Augustan practical criticism.
- Identify the shift toward historical and psychological criticism in the Romantic period.
- Explain the key concepts of major critical theories from antiquity to the early nineteenth century.
- Evaluate the strengths and limitations of different critical approaches to literature.
- Apply critical concepts to the analysis of literary texts from the periods studied.

## Detailed notes

### Plato's critique of poetry: mimesis and the Ion

Plato's critique of poetry is rooted in his broader philosophical project: the search for truth through reason. In *The Republic*, Plato argues that poetry is imitation (mimesis) of an imitation — the poet imitates the physical world, which itself is an imitation of the Forms. This double remove from truth makes poetry epistemologically suspect. Plato also argues that poetry appeals to the lower, emotional part of the soul and corrupts the rational control that the ideal citizen must cultivate. In the dialogue *Ion*, Plato questions the claim that the poet is inspired by divine madness, suggesting instead that the poet's inspiration is no different from the madness of the magnetiser who inspires the medium. These arguments have shaped the Western debate about the value and status of literature for over two millennia. Students should learn to articulate Plato's critique clearly and to evaluate its strengths and weaknesses.

### Aristotle's catharsis and the Poetics

Aristotle's *Poetics* (c. 335 BCE) is the foundational text of Western literary criticism. Aristotle counter-argues Plato: tragedy is not morally dangerous but therapeutically valuable, because it purges the emotions of pity and fear through catharsis. The plot — which Aristotle calls the 'soul of tragedy' — must be unified, probable and complete, with a beginning, middle and end that follow from one another (peripeteia, or reversal of fortune, and anagnorisis, or recognition). Aristotle's emphasis on structure and causation shaped the neoclassical unities and the later development of dramatic criticism. His concept of catharsis — the emotional purification or purgation that tragedy produces — remains one of the most debated ideas in literary theory. Students should learn to explain Aristotle's theory of tragedy and to apply its concepts (plot, character, thought, diction, melody, spectacle) to the analysis of dramatic texts.

### Sidney's Defence of Poesy

Philip Sidney's *The Defence of Poesy* (1595) is the Renaissance's most eloquent response to the ancient attacks on poetry. Sidney rebuts Plato's charge that poetry is false, arguing that the poet creates 'a golden world' that is more perfect than nature — poetry is not imitation but creation. Sidney argues that poetry is 'the most high, estimative, and sigmaticall of things', combining the moral instruction of philosophy, the delight of history and the persuasion of law in a single art form. The poet, for Sidney, is a maker (the word 'poet' comes from the Greek *poiesis*, meaning 'making') who uses imagination to reveal truths that philosophy can only argue about. Sidney's defence established the prestige of poetry as an intellectual and moral enterprise and influenced the development of English Renaissance literature. Students should learn to articulate Sidney's arguments and to compare them with Plato's critique.

### Dryden and Johnson: practical criticism

John Dryden (1631–1700) and Samuel Johnson (1709–1784) represent the tradition of practical criticism — close reading with judgment, evaluating specific works and authors against established standards. Dryden, as Poet Laureate, wrote prefaces and essays that set the terms for literary judgment in the Restoration and early Augustan periods. Johnson's *Lives of the Poets* (1779–1781) is the landmark of English critical biography: each life combines biographical information with close analysis of the poet's work and a judgment of its merits and faults. Johnson's practical criticism is characterised by its common sense, its attention to the reader's experience and its insistence that literature must be judged by its effects on the audience. Students should learn to identify the features of practical criticism and to compare Dryden's and Johnson's approaches.

### Wordsworth's Preface and Romantic theory

Wordsworth's Preface to the second edition of *Lyrical Ballads* (1800) is one of the most important texts in the history of literary criticism. Wordsworth argues that poetry is the 'spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings' and that the language of common life is the fittest medium for poetry. He defends the choice of subjects from humble life — rural people, their occupations, their conversations — on the grounds that these subjects allow 'the essential passions of the heart' to speak in their own language. Wordsworth's theory of poetic diction — his rejection of the 'arbitrary and capricious habit of expression' of Augustan poetic diction — was revolutionary. The Preface also introduces the concept of the poet as a man 'speaking to men', establishing a democratic, accessible vision of poetry that contrasts sharply with the elite, courtly tradition of the Renaissance and Augustan periods.

### Modern critical theory: an overview

The Unit concludes with an overview of the shift toward modern critical theory. The Romantic period opened the door to the personal, the historical and the psychological in literary criticism: Wordsworth's emphasis on the poet's imagination, Coleridge's theory of the imagination, and the emergence of biographical and historical criticism all paved the way for the twentieth century's theoretical movements. The modern turn — toward formalism, structuralism, post-structuralism, Marxism, feminism, postcolonialism and other approaches — extends the critical tradition that begins with Plato and Aristotle. Students should learn to identify the major schools of modern criticism and to understand how they relate to the classical and Renaissance foundations covered in the Unit. The Unit does not cover modern theory in depth but provides the historical grounding necessary for more advanced study.

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

### Short answer (moderate)
1. Explain Plato's critique of poetry in *The Republic*. Why does he consider poetry morally dangerous?
2. Define catharsis as Aristotle uses the term. What emotions does tragedy purge, and how?
3. What is Sidney's main argument in *The Defence of Poesy*? How does he rebut Plato?
4. What is practical criticism, and how do Dryden and Johnson exemplify it?
5. What are the key claims of Wordsworth's Preface to *Lyrical Ballads*?
6. How does Aristotle's concept of plot differ from a mere sequence of events?

### Essay questions (challenging)
1. 'Plato and Aristotle offer fundamentally different accounts of the value of poetry.' Compare and contrast their positions, using specific arguments from *The Republic* and the *Poetics*.
2. 'Sidney's Defence of Poesy established the intellectual prestige of poetry in the English Renaissance.' Discuss Sidney's main arguments and evaluate their significance.
3. How does Wordsworth's theory of poetic diction in the Preface to *Lyrical Ballads* represent a break with the Augustan tradition? Discuss with reference to specific passages.

### Objective / passage-based (moderate to challenging)
1. In the *Ion*, Plato argues that the poet's inspiration is similar to the madness of the (a) poet (b) magnetiser (c) philosopher (d) king.
2. Which of the following is NOT one of Aristotle's six elements of tragedy? (a) plot (b) character (c) rhyme (d) thought.
3. Passage analysis: Read the following from Sidney's *Defence of Poesy*: 'The poet, making nothing else, maketh swans and blackbirds.' What does Sidney mean by this?
4. Multiple choice: Wordsworth's Preface appeared in the (a) first edition of *Lyrical Ballads* (1798) (b) second edition (1800) (c) third edition (1802) (d) posthumous edition.
