# ENG 301 — Victorian Literature (Exam Study Guide)

## Unit overview

The Victorian period (1837–1901) witnessed the height of British imperial power alongside profound social dislocation: rapid industrialisation, mass urbanisation, and widening class divisions. Literature of the era is simultaneously confident in British progress and deeply anxious about its costs — about the erosion of faith following Darwin and geological discoveries, about the condition of the working classes in industrial cities, and about the role of women and the boundaries of the family. These tensions produced three dominant modes that this Unit asks you to compare: realism (which sought to “represent ordinary life” with scrupulous detail), sensationalism (which used mystery, scandal and disguise to explore moral danger), and the Gothic (which gave form to repressed fears about gender, foreignness and violence).

Three novelists anchor the realist tradition. **Charles Dickens** fused elaborate plotting with direct social indictment — his *Condition of England* novels such as *Hard Times* and *Bleak House* depict workhouses, fog-bound courts and the grinding poor law. **George Eliot** (Mary Ann Evans) brought psychological depth to provincial life in *Middlemarch*, using free indirect discourse to explore idealism frustrated by social reality. **Elizabeth Gaskell** wrote the industrial/social problem novel explicitly — *North and South* stages the conflict between mill owners and workers, while *Mary Barton* depicts the starvation of the 1840s.

The period’s poetry registers a parallel crisis of certainty. Tennyson’s *In Memoriam* moves from grief and doubt to Christian consolation, while Robert Browning’s dramatic monologues expose the speaker’s self-deception. Matthew Arnold’s “Dover Beach” laments the retreat of the “Sea of Faith.” The Unit also covers women’s writing — the Brontës’ gothic passion and Charlotte’s *Jane Eyre* reworking of female agency; the Rossettis and Browning in poetry. Finally, the imperial frame reaches its fullest expression in Rudyard Kipling’s later “plain Tales” and *Barrack-Room Ballads*, which give working‑soldier voice to empire while naturalising its hierarchy.

## Learning outcomes

- Distinguish realism, sensationalism and the Gothic as Victorian modes of representing social anxiety.
- Analyse how major novelists depict industrialisation, class and gender.
- Interpret Victorian poetry as response to scientific doubt and moral crisis.
- Evaluate the contribution of women writers to Victorian literary culture.
- Assess representations of empire and the dominion in Victorian writing.
- Construct close readings supported by historical and biographical context.

## Detailed notes

### The condition of England: industrial novel and social problem fiction

The phrase “Condition of England” names the Victorian preoccupation with describing industrial society. **Industrial (social problem) novels** such as Gaskell’s *North and South*, Dickens’s *Hard Times* (Coketown’s “fusees and cotton-spans”) and *The Condition of England* by G. M. Young use specific institutions — the factory, the workhouse, the court — to critique laissez‑faire politics. Key terms: **social determinism** (characters shaped by environment); **diagnostic mode** (detailing social ills); **moral earnestness**. In *North and South*, Gaskell stages a strike and a factory inspection, asking readers to sympathise with both workers’ hardship and mill-owner paternalism — a tension Kenyan readers recognise from contemporary labour disputes.

### Realism: Eliot, Dickens and free indirect discourse

Realism is the technique of presenting ordinary, middle‑class life with psychological verisimilitude. George Eliot’s *Middlemarch* exemplifies **psychological realism**: Dorothea Brooke’s idealism is frustrated because the world exceeds her moral map. Eliot’s great technical contribution is **free indirect discourse** — the narrator thought‑reporting a character’s feelings while retaining ironic distance (“Dorothea’s mind… was not used to be satisfied”). Compare Dickens’s **caricature and melodrama**: characters like Mr. Gradgrind (*Hard Times*) are types rather than individuals — their names and traits signal the author’s moral judgement directly.

### Gothic and sensation: concealment in the home

The Victorian Gothic (Charlotte Brontë, *Jane Eyre*, the “madwoman in the attic” Bertha Mason; Stoker’s *Dracula*) expresses anxieties about female sexuality, foreign invasion and the repressed returning. **Sensation fiction** — Wilkie Collins’s *The Woman in White* and *The Moonstone* — uses mystery, forgery and bigamy to explore identity and class disguise. Both modes rely on the **uncanny domestic**: secrets hidden behind drawing‑room propriety. Feminist critics read Bertha as the silenced female voice that the patriarchal house cannot contain — a reading echoed in contemporary Kenyan women’s writing such as Yvonne Adhiambo Owuor’s *Dust*, where the house itself is a site of unspoken violence.

### Victorian poetry: faith, doubt and the dramatic voice

Tennyson’s *In Memoriam* (133 short lyrics) models the Victorian negotiation of science and faith; its refrain “‘tis better to have loved and lost” makes grief itself a form of knowledge. **Robert Browning** perfected the **dramatic monologue**: the speaker addresses a silent listener and, in doing so, reveals his own psychology and moral blindness (e.g. “My Last Duchess”). **Matthew Arnold’s** “Dover Beach” translates the loss of religious certainty into landscape metaphor — the sea that once “fetched” the world with “hoary froth” now retreats, leaving “the grim Head but the crumble of things.” Arnold’s “scholar‑gentleman” stance influences later British poets including the Edwardians Kipling treats skeptically.

### Women’s writing: Brontës, Gaskell and the poetic Rossettis

Charlotte Brontë’s *Jane Eyre* (1847) broke new ground in giving a governess — a liminal, educated woman — a voice of moral and emotional equality. The **Brontë myth** (the Haworth parsonage, the sisters’ early deaths) turned the novel into a symbol of female intellectual force. **Christina Rossetti** (Goblin Market and Other Poems) used devotional and sensual imagery to explore female desire and sacrifice. **Elizabeth Gaskell**, biographer of Charlotte Brontë, wrote novels of manners and class conflict with sympathetic attention to women’s economic vulnerability — a concern central to contemporary Kenyan women’s literary production.

### Empire, dominion and the imperial romance: Kipling

The Victorian imagination turned outward to empire. Rudyard Kipling (1865–1936), born in Bombay, wrote both the **Imperial romance** (*Kim*) and the **“Plain Tales”** and *Barrack‑Room Ballads* in a demotic, soldier‑voice idiom. His poetry (“Barrack‑Room Ballads”) uses versicles and Anglo‑Indian slang to claim English as a world language spoken by “Tommy” and sepoys alike. The **White Man’s Burden** (1899) famously enjoins the United States to take up imperial duty — a text now read as the literary justification of colonial exploitation. Kenyan readers encounter Kipling critically, often through Ngũgĩ’s *Decolonising the Mind*, which cites him as the exemplar of coloniser discourse.

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

### Short answer
1. **(Easy)** Define *free indirect discourse* and give one example from *Middlemarch*.
2. **(Medium)** Explain how Dickens uses the description of Coketown to critique industrial society.
3. **(Medium)** What is the significance of the “madwoman in the attic” in *Jane Eyre*?
4. **(Hard)** Compare the treatment of faith/doubt in Tennyson’s *In Memoriam* and Arnold’s “Dover Beach.”

### Essay questions
1. **(Medium)** “The Victorian novel is the great novel of social reform.” Discuss with reference to *Hard Times*, *Middlemarch* and *North and South*.
2. **(Hard)** Analyse the Gothic element in *Jane Eyre* and evaluate its function as social commentary.
3. **(Hard)** How far does Victorian poetry constitute a response to the “crisis of faith” produced by Darwinism and empire?

### Objective / passage-based
1. **(Easy)** Which mode does Wilkie Collins’s *The Woman in White* exemplify?
   - A) Social realism B) Sensation C) Romance D) Epic
2. **(Medium)** “The Sea of Faith” retreats in which poem?
3. **(Medium)** Extract: “Whatever thing was loathed, / He fixed it and the image stuck.” Identify the speaker and explain the moral psychology at work.
