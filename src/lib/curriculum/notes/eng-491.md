## Unit overview

Psycholinguistics is the study of the psychological processes involved in language: comprehension, production, reading, writing, bilingualism, and language disorders, with direct implications for teaching. The unit equips students with an understanding of how language is acquired, processed, and sometimes disrupted, and how this knowledge can inform effective English and Literature teaching. Students learn about the stages of first language acquisition, the cognitive demands of reading and writing, the nature of bilingual development, and the factors — working memory, age, aptitude — that influence language learning outcomes.

The unit also surfaces the relevance of **Creative Writing** as a psycholinguistic activity: the processes of generating text involve comprehension, production, and the coordination of multiple cognitive systems. Understanding these processes helps teachers support students who struggle with writing and reading, and helps them design instruction that aligns with how the mind processes language. The unit prepares students to apply psycholinguistic insights to language teaching and assessment in the secondary classroom.

By the end of the unit, students should be able to explain models of language comprehension and production, describe developmental and individual differences in reading and writing, and apply psycholinguistic insights to language teaching and assessment.

## Learning outcomes

- Explain models of language comprehension and production.
- Describe developmental and individual differences in reading and writing.
- Apply psycholinguistic insights to language teaching and assessment.
- Outline the stages of first language acquisition and BGLA.
- Explain the relationship between working memory and language processing.
- Analyse reading disability (dyslexia) and its implications for teaching.

## Detailed notes

### First language acquisition

**First language acquisition (FLA)** is the process by which children acquire their native language. It follows a broadly predictable sequence across cultures and languages, suggesting an innate biological capacity for language. The stages of FLA include: the **pre-linguistic stage** (crying, cooing, babbling, 0–12 months), the **one-word stage** (holophrases, 12–18 months), the **two-word stage** (telegraphic speech, 18–24 months), and the **multi-word stage** (morphological and syntactic development, 24 months onwards). Children progress from understanding more than they can produce (receptive before expressive) to producing increasingly complex sentences. The **critical period hypothesis** (Lenneberg) proposes that there is a biologically determined window for language acquisition, after which native-like competence becomes difficult to achieve.

### Bilingual first language acquisition (BGLA)

**Bilingual first language acquisition (BGLA)** occurs when children are exposed to two languages from birth or early childhood. BGLA children follow the same developmental stages as monolingual children but may reach milestones at slightly different rates. Common phenomena include **language mixing** (using elements from both languages in a single utterance), **code-switching**, and **asymmetric proficiency** (being stronger in one language than the other). BGLA does not cause confusion: bilingual children develop both languages without interference, and the cognitive benefits of bilingualism — enhanced executive function, metalinguistic awareness — are well documented. The **age factor** in BGLA matters: early exposure (before age 6–7) is associated with more native-like pronunciation and greater fluency in both languages.

### Reading, working memory, and dyslexia

**Reading** involves three interacting processes: **orthographic processing** (recognising written words), **phonological processing** (decoding the sound system of language), and **semantic processing** (accessing meaning). The **simple view of reading** (Gough and Tunmer) proposes that reading comprehension is the product of decoding and linguistic comprehension: Reading = Decoding × Linguistic Comprehension. A learner who is strong in one component but weak in the other will struggle overall.

**Working memory** is the cognitive system that temporarily holds and manipulates information. It is essential for listening comprehension, reading, and writing — tasks that require holding incoming information while simultaneously processing it. Short-term memory limits affect listening and reading, particularly for learners processing complex texts or following multi-step instructions.

**Dyslexia** is a specific learning difficulty that affects phonological processing — the ability to map sounds to letters. Dyslexic readers struggle with decoding despite normal intelligence and adequate instruction. The implications for teaching are significant: early identification, systematic phonics instruction, multi-sensory approaches, and accommodations (extra time, audio texts) can support dyslexic learners. Teachers should be aware that dyslexia is not a marker of low intelligence but a difference in how the brain processes written language.

### Age factor and language learning

The **age factor** in language learning refers to the relationship between age of exposure and ultimate attainment. Early exposure generally gives native-like pronunciation and greater fluency, but older learners can achieve high levels of proficiency, particularly in explicit knowledge (grammar rules, vocabulary). The **affective filter** (Krashen) — the emotional barriers (anxiety, motivation, self-confidence) that affect language acquisition — interacts with age: younger learners may have lower anxiety but less metacognitive control, while older learners have more strategic knowledge but may be more self-conscious.

### Implications for teaching

Psycholinguistic insights have direct implications for English and Literature teaching. Effective teaching provides learners with rich, meaningful input, opportunities to produce language, and explicit attention to form within meaning-focused tasks. Teachers should be aware of individual differences in aptitude, motivation, anxiety, working memory, and age when planning instruction. For struggling readers, systematic phonics and multi-sensory approaches are essential. For bilingual learners, translanguaging practices and respect for home languages support acquisition. Assessment should be informed by psycholinguistic knowledge: reading tests should measure both decoding and comprehension, and writing assessments should consider the cognitive demands of text production.

### Creative Writing and ESP connections

**Creative Writing** as a psycholinguistic activity involves the coordination of comprehension (understanding models and mentor texts), production (generating original text), and the management of working memory (holding plot, character, and language in mind simultaneously). Understanding the psycholinguistics of writing helps teachers support students who struggle with the writing process — from generating ideas to organising text to editing for accuracy. **English for Specific Purposes (ESP)** also draws on psycholinguistic research: understanding how readers process academic or professional texts informs the teaching of ESP genres.

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

### Short answer

1. Describe the stages of first language acquisition, giving the approximate age range for each.
2. What is BGLA, and how does it differ from monolingual acquisition?
3. Explain the simple view of reading and its implications for struggling readers.
4. What is dyslexia, and how does it affect phonological processing?
5. Define working memory and explain its role in language comprehension and production.
6. What is the critical period hypothesis, and what evidence supports it?
7. How does the age factor influence language learning outcomes?

### Essay questions

8. 'Bilingual children do not experience confusion; they develop two languages simultaneously.' Discuss with reference to BGLA research.
9. How does understanding dyslexia inform effective teaching of reading in the secondary English classroom?
10. 'Working memory is the bottleneck of language processing.' Evaluate this claim with reference to reading and writing.
11. 'The age factor in language learning is not about ability but about opportunity.' Discuss.

### Objective / passage-based

12. (Difficulty: Easy) According to the simple view of reading, reading comprehension equals: (A) decoding + comprehension (B) decoding × comprehension (C) decoding − comprehension (D) comprehension ÷ decoding
13. (Difficulty: Medium) Read the following classroom observation: a 7-year-old bilingual learner mixes English and Kiswahili in a single sentence. Is this a sign of confusion? Explain using BGLA theory.
14. (Difficulty: Hard) Given a case study of a secondary student with dyslexia, design a psycholinguistically informed teaching strategy that addresses both decoding and comprehension challenges.
