## Unit overview

The short story is one of the most widely read—and most examined—forms in the B.Ed (Arts) English curriculum, precisely because it demands both close reading and clear argument. Where the novel can sustain subplots and large casts, the short story must achieve its effect through a single “moment” of recognition: a turn, a revelation, a shift in understanding. This Unit trains the skills the KCSE and university examinations expect: identifying point of view and focalisation, tracking how compression and structure shape meaning, and producing a focused literary essay on an unseen passage.

The reading list moves from the theory of the form (Poe’s “single sitting” and unity of effect; Chekhov’s authorial restraint) through modernist experiments in consciousness (Joyce, Mansfield) to a distinctly African engagement with the form — writers such as Grace Ogot, Meja Mwangi, and the contributors to *Told by an African* who rework oral storytelling techniques into the printed page. Kenyan classrooms particularly value the short story because it is teachable in a single lesson and because it models the concise, evidence-driven paragraph that examiners reward.

## Learning outcomes

- Define the short story’s formal characteristics: compression, epiphany, unity of effect, and the management of time.
- Analyse point of view, focalisation, and the presentation of consciousness in short fiction.
- Interpret how structure and ending shape a reader’s response.
- Compare short stories across British, Commonwealth, and African traditions.
- Write a close, structured literary response to an unseen passage.

## Detailed notes

### Compression and the single effect

Edgar Allan Poe argued that a short story must be readable “in the period of a single sitting” and must produce “a single sitting, a single mood, a single tone of feeling.” This principle of **unity of effect** means every detail must contribute to one dominant impression — unlike the novel’s capacity for digression and subplot. **Compression** is the means: writers do more with less, trusting the reader to fill gaps. The form rewards economy: a name, a gesture, an object can carry thematic weight far beyond its literal meaning.

### Epiphany and the moment of recognition

Most short stories pivot on a moment of **epiphany** — a sudden illumination that re-frames everything the reader has taken for granted. James Joyce described the writer’s task as forging “in the smithy of my own conscience” the unities of the short story, and he used the term epiphany for the revelation of the ordinary as extraordinary. Katherine Mansfield’s *The Garden Party* and *Bliss* turn seemingly trivial domestic moments into exposures of class, sexuality and self-deception.

### Point of view and focalisation

Because the story is brief, the management of **point of view** is critical. Writers move from **homodiegetic** (a narrator who is a participant) to **heterodiegetic** (an outside narrator), and from **internal** to **external** focalisation (who sees). Chekhov famously refused to interpret for the reader — “The reader must at times warm himself at the fire he has lit” — letting implication and detail do the work. **Free indirect style** lets the third-person narrator adopt a character’s voice while keeping distance, a technique central to modern short fiction.

### Structure and ending

The short-story arc is tight: a hook, a complication, a climax, and a final line that must feel both surprising and inevitable. Plot is usually minimal; **character is revealed through action and dialogue**, not extended interior description. The ending is the test of the craft: it should arise out of the material, not be imposed upon it. Saki (H.H. Munro) uses a sudden reversal; Chekhov’s endings refuse closure, leaving questions. African writers often circle back to oral structures — circularity, communal voice, the trickster — as in the work collected in *Told by an African* (Moore & Glass).

### African voices and the short story

African writers have used the short story to explore, with particular sharpness, colonial encounter, urban migration, and gender. Grace Ogot’s *The Promised Gift* and *The Stone of Ibira* blend traditional narrative with Christian and modern sensibilities; Meja Mwangi’s urban stories capture post-independence Nairobi; the anthology *Told by an African* brings together writers from across the continent in English. These stories often carry the rhythms of orature—proverb, call-and-response, and the communal frame—even in written form.

### Reading an unseen passage: exam strategy

Examiners first ask you to describe what happens and who is speaking, then to analyse language and structure, and finally to comment on the author’s effect. Begin with a one-line gist, then note the narrative perspective, any shift in tone, and at least two specific devices (a metaphor, a sound pattern, a structural choice). Always link to the question asked. Avoid plot summary beyond the first sentence; the mark is for analysis.

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

### Short answer

1. **(Easy)** What does Poe mean by the “single effect” principle?
2. **(Easy)** Distinguish **focalisation** from **point of view**.
3. **(Medium)** Explain how Chekhov’s reluctance to explain creates meaning in a short story.
4. **(Medium)** What is an “epiphany” in the reading of a short story? Give one example.
5. **(Hard)** How do African short stories in English draw on oral tradition? Use one named story to illustrate.

### Essay questions

1. **(Medium)** “The short story’s power lies in what it leaves unsaid.” Discuss with reference to **at least two** named stories.
2. **(Hard)** Compare the treatment of urban life in an African short story with that in a British modernist short story.
3. **(Hard)** How far is a short story’s meaning shaped more by its **narrator** than by its **events**?

### Objective / passage-based

1. **(Easy)** Which device is most associated with the short story’s brevity?
   - A) Subplot  B) Epiphany  C) Chronicle  D) Epic catalogue
2. **(Medium)** A passage that withholds the narrator’s explanation of a character’s motive is most likely employing:
   - A) Omniscience  B) Irony  C) Free indirect discourse  D) Authorial intrusion
