## Unit overview

The History of the English Language traces the development of English from its Indo-European and Germanic roots through Old, Middle and Early Modern English to the present day. The unit focuses on major phonological, morphological and lexical changes and their social causes, showing how historical forces — invasions, Christianity, printing, empire — shaped the language that Kenyan students now study and teach. For students of English and literature, the unit is essential preparation for reading Shakespeare, Chaucer and Old English poetry such as *Beowulf*, and for understanding why English spelling, vocabulary and idiom are so irregular.

The unit also connects language history to contemporary debates in Kenya: the role of English in education, the tension between English and local languages, and the politics of standard versus non-standard varieties. Understanding the history of English equips teachers to explain these issues to their students and to appreciate the linguistic diversity of their classrooms.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this unit, students should be able to:
- Trace the development of English through Old, Middle and Early Modern periods.
- Explain major sound changes such as the Great Vowel Shift and their consequences for spelling and pronunciation.
- Account for lexical and grammatical change in terms of social and historical factors.
- Analyse Old English and Middle English texts with the aid of glossaries and notes.
- Relate the history of English to contemporary issues of language and education in Kenya.
- Evaluate the relationship between standard English and World Englishes, including Kenyan English.

## Detailed notes

### Old English (450–1150)

English began with the Anglo-Saxon settlements of the fifth century, bringing a heavily inflected Germanic tongue with three grammatical genders and a complex case system. *Beowulf* (c. 1000) is the great literary monument. Verse was **accentual** (four stresses per line, split by a caesura). Christian missionaries from 597 onwards added Latin religious vocabulary; Viking invasions from the eighth century added Norse words such as *sky*, *skin*, *they*, *are* and *egg*. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle records the early history in the vernacular.

### Middle English (1150–1500)

The Norman Conquest (1066) made French the language of court, law and elite culture while English survived among ordinary people. Over three centuries, English absorbed thousands of French loanwords — especially in law, cuisine, art and governance — and underwent profound grammatical change: **inflectional endings** disappeared, so word order became the main indicator of grammatical relations. Chaucer wrote *The Canterbury Tales* in the London dialect of the late fourteenth century, establishing the linguistic foundation of modern English.

### Early Modern English (1500–1700)

The Renaissance brought a flood of Latin and Greek borrowings, especially in science, medicine and the humanities. The **Great Vowel Shift** (c. 1400–1700) changed the pronunciation of long vowels dramatically — the reason *time* and *name* no longer match their spelling. William Caxton's printing press (1476) and the King James Bible (1611) spread standardisation. Shakespeare and his contemporaries coined or popularised thousands of words still in use: *assassination*, *lonely*, *accommodation*, *eyeball*, *fashionable*.

### Modern English (1700–present)

The eighteenth century saw the rise of **prescriptive grammar** and the codification of standard English, influenced by Samuel Johnson's *Dictionary* (1755). The British Empire exported English worldwide, creating World Englishes in India, Nigeria, Kenya and beyond. In Kenya, English is an official language and the medium of instruction, but it coexists with Sheng, Swahili and dozens of indigenous languages. **Kenyan English** has its own distinctive phonological, lexical and syntactic features, reflecting local contact histories.

**Key terms:** Old English · Middle English · Early Modern English · inflections · Great Vowel Shift · loanword · standardisation · prescriptive grammar · World Englishes · Kenyan English

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

**Short answer**
1. What were the three main linguistic consequences of the Norman Conquest? (Easy)
2. Define the Great Vowel Shift and explain why it matters for English spelling. (Medium)
3. How did the printing press contribute to the standardisation of English? (Medium)

**Essay questions**
1. "The history of English is a history of borrowing, mixing and change." Discuss with reference to at least three periods. (Medium)
2. Evaluate the arguments for and against teaching standard English in Kenyan schools, with reference to the history of the language. (Hard)
3. Analyse how Shakespeare's vocabulary reflects the linguistic flux of Early Modern English. (Medium)

**Objective / passage-based**
1. Read the following Old English lines and answer the questions that follow. (Hard)
   Hwæt! Wé Gárdena in géardagum,
   þéodcyninga, þrym gefrúnon.
   a. Translate these lines into Modern English. (2 marks)
   b. Identify two grammatical features that show the inflectional nature of Old English. (4 marks)
   c. How does the metre of this passage differ from Modern English verse? (2 marks)
