## Unit overview

This Unit builds the practical repertoire every English/Literature teacher needs in Kenyan secondary classrooms: moving from decoding print to reading for understanding, from modelling paragraph writing to guiding students through unseen poetry and prose, and from one-off lessons to whole-term set-book programmes. It is explicitly tied to the KCSE English (English A) and Literature papers, which require students to handle both “seen” texts and unseen passages confidently.

Because most Kenyan learners study in English as an additional language, the Unit gives priority to vocabulary development, mother-tongue support, and the integration of reading, writing, speaking and listening — the four strata of the *CEFR* communicative framework. It also addresses differentiation for mixed-ability classes, the principles of Assessment for Learning (peer and self-marking, formative feedback), and the pedagogic value of discussing controversial literature such as *Things Fall Apart* in a culturally responsive way.

## Learning outcomes

- Apply stage-by-stage reading comprehension strategies (before, during, after) to literary and informational texts.
- Teach vocabulary systematically using context, word study, and tiered academic language.
- Guide students through the writing process for narrative, descriptive, expository and literary-essay genres.
- Prepare students for KCSE unseen poetry/prose passages using scaffolded strategies.
- Design differentiated, inclusive lessons drawing on Kiswahili and mother-tongue resources.

## Detailed notes

### Reading comprehension: the three-phase model

Effective reading instruction moves through three phases. **Before reading**: activate prior knowledge, predict content, and pre-teach key vocabulary — especially important for learners reading in a second language. **During reading**: use overt strategies — questioning, summarising, clarifying — and model think-alouds. **After reading**: discuss themes, make connections to experience, and recycle language through speaking/writing tasks. The *SQ3Q* or *PQ4R* frameworks give students a checklist; *reciprocal teaching* (teacher and pupils take turns leading a summary–question–clarification–prediction dialogue) is especially powerful with set texts.

### Vocabulary and the tiers

Not all words need equal attention. *Tier 2* words (high-utility academic words such as *contrast*, *evaluate*, *infer*) appear across subjects and deserve direct teaching; *Tier 1* are everyday spoken words; *Tier 3* are subject-specific (*soliloquy*, *simile*, *iambic*). Pre-teach Tier 3 words before reading a poem; teach Tier 2 words incidentally and through context. Use the *morphology* of words (roots, prefixes, suffixes) to unlock meaning — useful for learners whose first languages differ in word-structure.

### Writing across genres

Students must write in narrative, descriptive, expository, argumentative, and literary-critical forms. The **process approach** (pre-writing → drafting → peer feedback → revising → editing → publishing) works across genres. Teach **paragraphing explicitly**: a topic sentence, supporting evidence (a quotation for literature essays), and analysis that links back to the point. A literary essay answer to a KCSE set-text question should follow **P.E.E.L.** (Point, Evidence, Explanation, Link). Give specific, actionable feedback (“Add one more reason here” rather than “Good”).

### Unseen poetry and prose: exam strategy

For unseen poetry, train students first to read the title and annotate the poem for rhyme, rhythm and key imagery; then identify the speaker, persona and tone; then pick **two** clear points and support each with at least one quotation and explanation. For unseen prose, the skill is selecting the **best** evidence (not the longest), identifying narrative technique (point of view, focalisation), and always tying effect to the question. Time management: rough planning (3–4 minutes), writing (25–30 minutes), review (3–4 minutes).

### Set-book discussion and literature pedagogy

Approaches such as **shared reading** (teacher and class read aloud together), **dramatised reading**, and **reader-response discussion** build confidence with demanding texts like *Macbeth* or *Things Fall Apart*. Use **role-on-the-wall**, **hot-seating**, and **freeze-frame** activities to make characters real. Always connect literary ideas to students’ own lives and contemporary Kenya — the migrant experience in *Song of Lawino*, urban alienation, family structures — so the “foreign” text becomes discussable.

### Assessment for Learning and differentiation

Mixed-ability classes need **differentiation**: tiered tasks (different lengths of planning), choice boards (pick your question or genre), and scaffolding (sentence starters, word banks). **Formative assessment** — peer review, oral presentations, quick quizzes — should inform the summative mark, not replace it. Use **rubrics** that separate content, organisation and language, and share them with students before they begin.

### Teaching sensitively: controversial literature

Texts such as *Things Fall Apart*, *A Doll’s House*, or poetry about gender and violence can provoke strong reactions. Establish ground rules (“challenge ideas, not people”), check whether a learner needs to opt out for cultural reasons, and always link controversial content to lived experience with care. Professional development — peer observation, small-scale action research, and membership of communities such as KELA (Kenya English Language Association) — keeps practice growing.

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

### Short answer

1. **(Easy)** What are the three phases of a reading lesson according to the before–during–after model?
2. **(Easy)** Classify these words by tier: *run*, *analyse*, *metaphor*.
3. **(Medium)** Outline the steps of the writing process and explain how peer feedback fits in.
4. **(Medium)** When answering an unseen poetry question, what two things must every paragraph contain?
5. **(Hard)** Give three ways you would differentiate a lesson on a KCSE set text for a mixed-ability class.

### Essay questions

1. **(Medium)** “Close reading and explicit vocabulary teaching are the two pillars of literature instruction in Kenyan secondary schools.” Discuss.
2. **(Hard)** Design a 40-minute lesson plan (objectives, activities, differentiation, assessment) for teaching one poem from the KCSE set-text list to Form 3 students.
3. **(Hard)** How can formative assessment be used to improve KCSE Literature performance without narrowing the curriculum?

### Objective / passage-based

1. **(Easy)** In the P.E.E.L. structure for a literature answer, what does the “L” stand for?
   - A) Learning outcome  B) Link  C) Literature  D) Line
2. **(Medium)** Which strategy best supports reading comprehension *before* reading?
   - A) Summarising  B) Predicting  C) Questioning  D) Clarifying
