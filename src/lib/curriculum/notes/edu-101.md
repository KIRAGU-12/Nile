## Unit overview

Communication Skills and Academic Writing forms the methodological backbone of the first semester, ensuring that students can produce the coherent, evidence-based prose required in literary studies and beyond. The unit moves from the broad architecture of the essay — thesis, structure, paragraphing — to sentence-level precision, source integration and referencing conventions. For Kenyan students, many of whom are navigating academic English as a second or additional language, the unit offers practical remedies for common errors in article use, subject-verb agreement and register. Mastery of the Harvard referencing system is not merely a bureaucratic requirement; it is a professional skill expected in every Kenyan university and by most employers.

The unit also foregrounds the ethical dimension of academic writing: the distinction between paraphrase, summary, quotation and plagiarism. Because literary essays depend on close quotation of set texts, students must learn to embed quotations smoothly, analyse them explicitly, and avoid the common trap of "dropping in" quotation without comment. By the end of the semester, students should be able to draft, revise and proofread an essay of approximately 2,400 words that presents a debatable claim, marshals textual evidence and acknowledges sources correctly.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this unit, students should be able to:
- Plan, draft and revise essays that present a clear, organised and debatable argument.
- Construct unified paragraphs with clear topic sentences, integrated evidence and coherent transitions.
- Integrate and acknowledge sources using the Harvard (author-date) referencing system.
- Identify and correct common grammatical and mechanical errors in academic prose.
- Distinguish between paraphrase, summary, quotation and plagiarism.
- Apply academic style appropriate to literary and humanities writing.

## Detailed notes

### Academic essay structure

A literary essay advances a debatable claim — a thesis — supported by evidence and analysis. The introduction orients the reader, supplies necessary context and states the thesis. Each body paragraph turns on a single point (topic sentence), offers evidence (usually a short quotation from the set text) and explains how the evidence supports the point. The conclusion does not merely repeat the argument; it evaluates its significance or opens a new question. Unity (one idea per paragraph), coherence (logical flow) and proportion (neither over-detailed nor thin) are essential. An essay of approximately 2,400 words typically requires an introduction of 200–250 words, three body sections of 600–700 words each, and a conclusion of 200–250 words.

### The paragraph

A good paragraph has three movements: a topic sentence, evidence and explanation. The topic sentence states the paragraph's argument; the evidence supplies a concrete instance (a quotation, a reference to a scene, a statistic); the explanation shows how the evidence proves the point. Keep one idea per paragraph. Use transitional words and phrases — however, furthermore, in contrast, consequently — to link paragraphs and signal the argument's direction. Avoid paragraphs that simply narrate plot; always move toward interpretation.

### Using sources

Always cite sources for both ideas and direct quotation. When you summarise or paraphrase, still give credit. **Plagiarism** — presenting others' ideas or words as your own — is a serious academic offence in Kenyan universities. Harvard in-text citation takes the form (Author Year, page). The reference list at the end gives surname, initials, year, *italicised title*, edition, place: publisher. For example: Achebe, C. (1958), *Things Fall Apart*. London: Heinemann.

### Common errors and revision strategies

Common problems include run-on sentences and fragments; missing articles (a, an, the) and subject-verb disagreement; quotations dropped in without analysis; and conclusions that only summarise instead of evaluating. A reliable revision checklist asks: Does every paragraph have a topic sentence? Is each quotation introduced and explained? Does the conclusion go beyond summary? Are all sources cited? Reading the essay aloud reveals awkward syntax and missing transitions.

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

**Short answer**
1. Rewrite the following as a single, coherent thesis statement: "In *The Concubine*, Elechi Amadi uses imagery to create mood." (Easy)
2. What is the difference between summary and paraphrase? Give an example of each using a sentence from a set text. (Easy)
3. Explain why a topic sentence should be arguable rather than merely descriptive. (Medium)

**Essay questions**
1. "The conclusion of a literary essay should evaluate rather than merely summarise." Discuss with reference to essay structure. (Medium)
2. Analyse how paragraphing, evidence and explanation work together in one body paragraph of an essay you have written this semester. (Hard)
3. "Plagiarism is a failure of academic ethics, not just a technical error." Discuss. (Medium)

**Objective / passage-based**
1. Identify the error in each of the following sentences and correct it. (Easy)
   a. The students was reading *The River Between* when the power went off. (1 mark)
   b. Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, he is a major East African writer. (1 mark)
   c. The novel explores themes of colonialism, identity, and tradition. (1 mark)
2. Rewrite the following paragraph so that it is unified and analytical rather than narrative: "In Chapter 3 of *A Grain of Wheat*, Mugo goes to the threshing ground. He thinks about his past. He sees Kihika's ghost. The chapter shows how history haunts the present." (Hard)
