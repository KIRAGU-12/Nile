## Unit overview

Introduction to Drama introduces students to the unique demands of reading, interpreting and analysing plays — literature written for performance. The unit covers theatrical vocabulary, dramatic structure, characterisation through dialogue and the relationship between text and stage. For Kenyan students, the unit validates dramatic traditions beyond the Western canon, from community theatre to political stagecraft, while also building the analytical skills required for the KCSE Literature Paper 3 and for university coursework. It examines how drama creates meaning not only through words but through space, time, gesture and theatrical convention.

The unit also addresses practical aspects of drama teaching: reading a play as a blueprint for performance, understanding stage directions, and imagining how dialogue reveals status, intention and conflict. By the end of the semester, students should be able to analyse how dramatic structure builds and releases tension, how character is revealed through speech and action, and how theatrical conventions shape audience response.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this unit, students should be able to:
- Use basic theatrical vocabulary: proscenium, aside, soliloquy, chorus, catastrophe, dramatic irony.
- Analyse how dramatic structure creates tension and release through exposition, rising action, climax and resolution.
- Interpret how character is revealed through dialogue, stage directions and action.
- Evaluate the relationship between text and performance in a given play.
- Compare theatrical conventions across periods and traditions, including Elizabethan, Restoration and modern drama.
- Plan and deliver a short performance or dramatic reading, reflecting on interpretive choices.

## Detailed notes

### Dramatic form and structure

Drama is literature in dialogue intended for performance. Its meaning is made through speech, action, voice, movement, space and time — not just the printed word. Classical tragedy follows five parts: **exposition**, **rising action**, **climax**, **falling action** and **catastrophe** (the final resolution). Aristotle's **unities** of action, time and place were ideals rather than strict rules. Devices include the **soliloquy** (a character alone, revealing inner thought), the **aside** (spoken to the audience, unheard by other characters), **dramatic irony** (the audience knows more than the characters) and the **chorus** (commentary on the action, especially in Greek tragedy).

### Stagecraft and interpretation

When reading a play, imagine it staged. Note entrances and exits, spatial relationships, timing of dialogue and the relationship between spoken words and **stage directions**. Consider how costume, props and set convey status, theme and psychological state. Theatrical conventions change: the Elizabethan thrust stage placed actors close to the audience; the Restoration **proscenium** created a picture-frame effect; **naturalism** aimed to replicate real life; Brecht's **alienation** effect prevented emotional identification to promote critical distance. Each convention shapes what the audience feels and thinks.

### Characterisation through dialogue

In drama, character is revealed almost entirely through what people say and do. Pay attention to speech patterns: formality or informality, interruptions, silences, pauses. What is left unsaid can be as revealing as what is spoken. **Subtext** — the meaning beneath the words — is crucial. In Shakespeare, differences in verse and prose signal social status; in modern drama, silence and repetition can convey trauma or resistance.

### Drama in Kenya and Africa

Kenyan drama has a rich history: from community-based *hadithi* and ritual performance to postcolonial political theatre by groups such as Phoenix Players and Mombasa Little Theatre. Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o's plays, such as *The Trial of Dedan Kimathi*, blend oral storytelling with political critique. Contemporary Kenyan playwrights such as Shalini Gubili and Victor Ghai explore urban identity, gender and diaspora. Understanding these traditions enriches the reading of canonical Western drama by highlighting global theatrical practices.

**Key terms:** proscenium · aside · soliloquy · chorus · catastrophe · dramatic irony · stage directions · subtext · unity (action, time, place) · naturalism · alienation effect · exposition · rising action · climax · falling action

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

**Short answer**
1. Define "dramatic irony" and give an example from *Hamlet* or any play studied in class. (Easy)
2. What is the function of a chorus in Greek tragedy? (Easy)
3. Explain the difference between a soliloquy and an aside. (Medium)

**Essay questions**
1. "In drama, character is revealed through speech and action rather than authorial description." Discuss with reference to two plays. (Medium)
2. Analyse how theatrical convention shapes audience response in one production or performance you have studied. (Hard)
3. Compare the treatment of tragedy in Shakespeare's *Macbeth* and a modern African play. (Medium)

**Objective / passage-based**
1. Read the following extract from *Hamlet* and answer the questions that follow. (Hard)
   a. Identify the dramatic device used and explain its effect on the audience. (4 marks)
   b. How does the speaker's diction reveal his emotional state? (3 marks)
   c. Comment on the relationship between the speaker's words and the stage directions. (3 marks)
