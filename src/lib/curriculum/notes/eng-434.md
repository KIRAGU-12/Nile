## Unit overview

Literary Stylistics is the systematic linguistic analysis of literary texts, providing a precise vocabulary for discussing how form creates meaning. The unit equips students with the tools to move beyond impressionistic criticism to evidence-based readings of prose, poetry, and drama. By examining foregrounding, deviation, and redundancy, students learn to identify the linguistic choices that make literary language distinctive and how those choices shape the reader's experience.

The unit covers the core areas of stylistic analysis: **focalisation** (who sees the story), **speech and thought presentation** (how characters' inner lives are rendered on the page), and **prosody** (the sound patterns of poetry). An introduction to **corpus stylistics** extends the analytical toolkit by showing how large databases can reveal patterns invisible to the naked eye. The unit bridges linguistics and literary study, preparing students for both advanced literary analysis and the teaching of language and literature in secondary schools.

By the end of the unit, students should be able to apply linguistic concepts to the analysis of literary texts, analyse point of view and focalisation in fiction, interpret sound and prosody in poetry, and use corpus tools to investigate stylistic patterns. The unit also surfaces the relevance of stylistics for teaching English and for understanding how literary texts work at the level of language.

## Learning outcomes

- Apply linguistic concepts (e.g. deviation, foregrounding, cohesion) to the analysis of literary texts.
- Analyse point of view, focalisation, and speech/thought presentation in fiction using established frameworks.
- Interpret sound and prosody in poetic texts, including metre, rhyme, and enjambment.
- Use corpus stylistic methods (keyword analysis, collocation) to identify distinctive patterns in literary texts.
- Evaluate the strengths and limitations of stylistic analysis as a critical approach.
- Teach stylistic concepts effectively in the secondary English classroom.

## Detailed notes

### What is stylistics?

**Stylistics** is the linguistic analysis of literary (and sometimes non-literary) texts. It asks how form creates meaning at the level of grammar, lexis, sound, and structure. The aim is not to 'explain away' literature but to add a precise vocabulary for talking about how it works. Stylistics draws on the tools of modern linguistics — phonology, grammar, semantics, pragmatics — and applies them systematically to literary texts. The foundational text is Leech and Short's *Style in Fiction*, which provides a comprehensive framework for analysing literary prose.

### Foregrounding

**Foregrounding** is the central concept of stylistics: literary language stands out from ordinary language through two mechanisms. **Deviation** is the unexpected — archaism, neologism, unusual collocations, or the breaking of grammatical norms. **Redundancy** is the repeated — parallelism, lists, anaphora, and other patterns that draw attention to themselves. When a reader notices a pattern being broken or a pattern being insistently repeated, they have encountered foregrounding. The effect is to slow reading down, to make the reader attend to language rather than simply decode content.

### Focalisation and point of view

**Focalisation** refers to the angle from which a story is seen. Genette distinguished three dimensions: **mood** (who sees: internal focalisation, where the reader is restricted to a character's perceptions, or external, where the narrator sees from outside), **voice** (who speaks: homodiegetic narrator, a character in the story, or heterodiegetic narrator, outside the story), and **time** (order, duration, frequency of narration). **Free indirect discourse** is a key technique: it combines internal focalisation (the character's thoughts) with the narrator's voice, creating a blended perspective that is neither fully the character's nor fully the narrator's. This technique is a hallmark of the English novel, used by Austen, the Brontës, George Eliot, and Woolf.

### Speech and thought presentation

**Speech and thought presentation** maps the gradient from reported to direct forms. Fowler's model (Types 0–4) charts this: Type 0 is **reported thought** ('He thought that she was lying'), Type 1 is **direct thought** ('I know she's lying'), Type 2 is **free direct thought** (the character's words without quotation marks or reporting clause), Type 3 is **free indirect thought** (the character's thoughts blended with the narrator's voice), and Type 4 is **reported speech** ('He said that she was lying'). Most 19th- and 20th-century fiction moves fluidly across these types, and the choice of type shapes the reader's access to characters' inner lives.

### Corpus stylistics

**Corpus stylistics** uses large electronic databases — such as the Hansard Corpora, the Cambridge English Corpus, or Google Books Ngram — to find statistically significant patterns in literary texts. **Keyword analysis** identifies words that occur more frequently in a text than expected in a reference corpus, revealing thematic or stylistic emphases. **Collocation analysis** examines which words tend to appear together, uncovering semantic fields and patterns of association. Corpus stylistics does not replace close reading but complements it, providing quantitative evidence for patterns that close reading may have detected intuitively.

### Sound in poetry

In poetry, **prosody** — the patterning of stress, rhythm, and sound — carries meaning beyond the denotation of words. **Metre** is the regular pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables (e.g. iambic pentameter). **Rhyme** (end rhyme, internal rhyme, slant rhyme) creates sonic unity and emphasis. **Alliteration** (repetition of initial consonant sounds) and **assonance** (repetition of vowel sounds) create texture and can reinforce or counterpoint semantic content. **Enjambment** (the continuation of a sentence across a line break) creates tension between the line and the sentence, slowing or accelerating the reader's pace. Reading aloud is essential to understanding how sound shapes meaning in poetry.

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

### Short answer

1. Define foregrounding and explain the two mechanisms (deviation and redundancy) with one example each.
2. What is the difference between homodiegetic and heterodiegetic narration?
3. Explain free indirect discourse and identify one text where it is used prominently.
4. Describe Fowler's Type 3 speech presentation with an example.
5. What is keyword analysis in corpus stylistics, and what does it reveal?
6. Define enjambment and explain its effect on the reader's experience of a poem.

### Essay questions

7. 'Stylistics restores the importance of language to literary study.' Discuss with reference to at least two concepts from the unit.
8. Compare the use of free indirect discourse in two novels studied on the unit, showing how it shapes the reader's relationship to the characters.
9. How does corpus stylistics complement traditional close reading? Use a specific example to illustrate your argument.
10. Analyse the role of sound patterns (alliteration, assonance, metre) in a poem of your choice, showing how they contribute to meaning.

### Objective / passage-based

11. (Difficulty: Easy) Identify the type of speech presentation in the following: 'She was sure he was lying.' (Options: reported thought, direct thought, free indirect thought, reported speech)
12. (Difficulty: Medium) Given a passage of free indirect discourse, identify where the character's voice blends with the narrator's and explain the effect.
13. (Difficulty: Hard) Given a corpus stylistics output showing keyword frequencies for a novel, explain what the pattern reveals about the text's thematic concerns.
