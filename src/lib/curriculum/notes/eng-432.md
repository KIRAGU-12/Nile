## Unit overview

Pragmatics is the study of how meaning is constructed in context. The unit examines how speakers and writers do things with language — not just what they say, but what they mean, how they manage social relationships, and how context shapes interpretation. Students learn to analyse utterances in terms of speech acts, conversational implicature, deixis, and politeness, and to apply these concepts to both everyday discourse and literary texts.

The unit bridges linguistics and literary study by showing how pragmatic concepts illuminate the interpretation of dialogue, narrative voice, and reader positioning in fiction. Students explore how authors exploit the gap between literal and intended meaning — the territory of irony, metaphor, and indirection — and how readers draw on contextual knowledge to infer what is meant. The unit also introduces discourse analysis as a method for examining how language constructs social realities, with applications to Kenyan contexts of multilingual communication.

By the end of the unit, students should be able to analyse utterances in terms of speech act and conversational implicature, identify deictic expressions and politeness strategies in discourse, and apply pragmatic concepts to interpret literary dialogue. The unit also surfaces the relevance of pragmatics for English for Specific Purposes (ESP), where understanding context-dependent meaning is essential for effective communication in professional and academic settings.

## Learning outcomes

- Analyse utterances in terms of speech act and conversational implicature.
- Identify deictic expressions and politeness strategies in discourse.
- Apply pragmatic concepts to interpret literary dialogue.
- Distinguish between locutionary, illocutionary, and perlocutionary acts.
- Explain Grice's cooperative principle and the four maxims with examples.
- Analyse how pragmatics operates in multilingual Kenyan discourse contexts.

## Detailed notes

### Speech acts

J.L. Austin's speech act theory distinguishes three levels of action in language use. The **locutionary act** is what is said — the literal meaning of the utterance. The **illocutionary act** is what is done in saying it — the speaker's intention (e.g. promising, warning, requesting, apologising). The **perlocutionary act** is the effect the utterance has on the listener (e.g. persuading, frightening, motivating). John Searle classified illocutionary acts into five categories: **assertives** (committing the speaker to a proposition), **directives** (attempting to get the hearer to do something), **commissives** (committing the speaker to future action), **expressives** (expressing psychological states), and **declarations** (changing the reality by speaking, e.g. 'I now pronounce you...').

### Grice and implicature

H.P. Grice's **cooperative principle** holds that participants in conversation normally make their contributions such as is required, at the stage at which it occurs, by the accepted purpose or direction of the talk exchange. Grice identified four **maxims**: **Quantity** (make your contribution as informative as required), **Quality** (do not say what you believe to be false or for which you lack evidence), **Relation** (be relevant), and **Manner** (be perspicuous, avoid ambiguity and obscurity). When a speaker appears to violate a maxim, listeners infer an implied meaning — a **conversational implicature**. Irony and metaphor exploit the gap between literal and intended meaning: when a speaker says 'What a beautiful day' during a storm, the maxim of Quality is deliberately flouted, and the implicature of sarcasm is derived.

### Deixis

**Deixis** is 'pointing' in language — the way expressions orient the utterance relative to the speaker, the time of utterance, and the place of utterance. **Person deixis** (I/you, he/she/they) positions participants in the discourse. **Temporal deixis** (now, then, yesterday, tomorrow) locates events in time relative to the moment of speaking. **Spatial deixis** (here, there, this, that) locates events in place. **Social deixis** encodes the social relationship between participants — the choice of 'you' vs. 'your Honour', 'tu' vs. 'vous' in French, or the use of titles in English. In literary texts, deixis shapes the reader's positioning relative to the narrative world and can signal shifts in focalisation or narrative distance.

### Politeness

Brown and Levinson's politeness theory distinguishes two kinds of **face**: **positive face** (the desire to be liked and admired) and **negative face** (the desire to be unimpeded and autonomous). Face-threatening acts (FTAs) — requests, criticisms, apologies — threaten one or both kinds of face. Speakers manage FTAs using **hedges** (softening devices, e.g. 'I wonder if...', 'Perhaps...'), **boosters** (intensifiers that strengthen the face-threatening force, e.g. 'I absolutely insist'), **off-record strategies** (implying rather than stating directly), and **apologising** or **declaring interest**. In literary dialogue, politeness strategies reveal character relationships, power dynamics, and social tensions.

### Discourse analysis

**Discourse analysis** examines how language functions in social contexts, looking beyond the sentence to the structures of conversation, narratives, and texts. It studies cohesion (how sentences are linked through reference, conjunction, and lexical chains), coherence (how meaning is organised across a text), and turn-taking in conversation. In the context of Kenyan multilingualism, discourse analysis reveals how speakers navigate between English, Kiswahili, and mother tongues, using code-switching and translanguaging to construct identity and manage social relationships.

### Pragmatics of literary texts

Pragmatics enriches the interpretation of literary texts by drawing attention to how characters' utterances do more than convey information. When a character in a novel says 'I'm fine' in response to a question about their wellbeing, the pragmatic analysis considers whether this is a literal assertion or a face-saving device, whether it violates the maxim of Quantity (saying too little), and what the implicature reveals about the character's emotional state. Irony in dialogue — where the literal meaning contradicts the intended meaning — is a pragmatic phenomenon that depends on the reader's ability to infer the speaker's implicature. Metaphor, too, is a pragmatic achievement: the speaker invites the hearer to understand one concept in terms of another, creating new meaning through contextual inference.

### ESP and Creative Writing connections

Pragmatics is foundational to **English for Specific Purposes (ESP)**, where learners must understand how language operates in specific contexts — academic writing (hedging, politeness in requests), business communication (directives, commissives), and professional correspondence. In **Creative Writing**, pragmatic awareness helps writers craft dialogue that sounds natural, reveals character, and advances plot. Writers must understand how characters' speech acts and politeness strategies reflect their social positions and psychological states.

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

### Short answer

1. Distinguish between locutionary, illocutionary, and perlocutionary acts with an example of each.
2. What are Grice's four maxims? Give a brief example of each.
3. Define conversational implicature and explain how it differs from entailment.
4. Identify three types of deixis and give an example of each from a literary text.
5. Explain the difference between positive and negative face in Brown and Levinson's theory.
6. What is a face-threatening act, and how do speakers mitigate it?
7. How does irony exploit the gap between literal and intended meaning?

### Essay questions

8. 'Pragmatics is essential for understanding how literature works at the level of dialogue.' Discuss with reference to at least two texts.
9. Compare Grice's theory of implicature with Brown and Levinson's politeness theory. Which is more useful for analysing literary dialogue?
10. How does deixis shape the reader's experience of a narrative? Use examples from a novel studied on the unit.
11. 'The pragmatics of literary texts reveals what characters cannot say directly.' Evaluate this claim.

### Objective / passage-based

12. (Difficulty: Easy) Identify the speech act type in the following utterance: 'I promise to finish the assignment by Friday.' (Options: assertive, directive, commissive, expressive)
13. (Difficulty: Medium) Read the following exchange and identify the implicature: 'Can you reach the shelf?' 'I'm only five feet tall.'
14. (Difficulty: Hard) Given a passage of literary dialogue containing a face-threatening act, analyse the politeness strategies used and explain what they reveal about the characters' relationship.
