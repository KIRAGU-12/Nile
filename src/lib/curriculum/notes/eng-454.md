## Unit overview

Multilingualism in the Kenyan Context examines the theories and practice of multilingualism with special reference to Kenya. The unit explores how speakers navigate multiple languages in everyday life — in homes, markets, schools, and digital spaces — and how these practices shape identity, social relationships, and educational outcomes. Students learn to analyse code-switching, translanguaging, urban youth language (Sheng), and the education system as a multilingual environment, developing a critical understanding of multilingualism as a resource rather than a problem.

The unit also surfaces the relevance of **Creative Writing** as a multilingual practice: writers in Kenya draw on multiple languages and registers to create texts that reflect the linguistic realities of their communities. Understanding multilingualism is essential for English and Literature teachers, who must navigate a classroom where students bring diverse linguistic repertoires and where the boundaries between languages are constantly negotiated.

By the end of the unit, students should be able to describe code-switching and translanguaging phenomena in Kenyan contexts, analyse the structure and social meaning of urban youth language (Sheng), and evaluate multilingual practices in Kenyan education.

## Learning outcomes

- Describe code-switching and translanguaging phenomena in Kenyan contexts.
- Analyse the structure and social meaning of urban youth language (Sheng).
- Evaluate multilingual practices in Kenyan education.
- Distinguish between code-switching and translanguaging as distinct but related concepts.
- Explain the social functions of Sheng and youth language in Kenyan society.
- Assess the role of digital communication in shaping multilingual practices.

## Detailed notes

### Code-switching

**Code-switching** is the alternation between two or more languages within an utterance, conversation, or text. In Kenya, English–Kiswahili and Kiswahili–mother-tongue switches are routine in everyday communication, media, and even formal settings. Code-switching follows systematic rules: the **matrix language frame** model (Myers-Scotton) proposes that one language provides the grammatical frame while the other contributes content words. **Markedness** determines which language is more 'marked' (less expected) in a given context. Code-switching is not random or lazy; it is a skilled communicative practice that indexes identity, solidarity, and stance. In Kenyan classrooms, code-switching between English and Kiswahili is common among both teachers and learners, and its pedagogical implications are debated — some see it as a scaffold for learning, others as a barrier to English proficiency.

### Translanguaging

**Translanguaging** is a more integrated practice than code-switching: it involves drawing flexibly on a speaker's full linguistic repertoire as one resource, rather than treating languages as separate, bounded systems. The translanguaging framework (Cen Williams, Li Wei) sees multilingual speakers as having a single, integrated linguistic repertoire from which they select features as needed. In Kenyan contexts, translanguaging describes how speakers move fluidly between English, Kiswahili, and mother tongues without perceiving these as separate 'codes'. Translanguaging challenges the assumption that monolingualism is the norm and validates multilingual competence as a cognitive and communicative asset. In the classroom, translanguaging practices can support learning by allowing students to use their full linguistic resources to make meaning.

### Sheng and urban youth language

**Sheng** is an evolving urban youth language that blends English, Kiswahili, and elements of ethnic languages (e.g. 'Ninakwenda sokoni' — 'I'm going to the market'). It emerged in Nairobi's urban neighbourhoods and has spread through music, media, and social networks. Sheng indexes youth identity and belonging across ethnic lines, functioning as a marker of urban, contemporary Kenyan identity. Its lexicon and grammar are constantly evolving, with new terms emerging and old ones falling out of use. Sheng is not a 'broken' form of English or Kiswahili but a legitimate linguistic variety with its own systematicity. For English teachers, Sheng raises questions about the relationship between students' home language practices and the standard English expected in school.

### Digital communication and multilingualism

Social media and digital communication have transformed multilingual practices in Kenya. **SMS abbreviations**, **hashtags**, and **mixed-language posts** on platforms like Twitter, WhatsApp, and Facebook reflect and extend the multilingual repertoire of Kenyan users. Digital communication allows speakers to draw on multiple languages simultaneously — mixing English, Kiswahili, and Sheng in a single message — and to create new forms of linguistic expression (emoji, memes, GIFs) that transcend traditional language boundaries. These digital practices are legitimate linguistic resources, not 'broken' language, and they reflect the creative adaptability of multilingual speakers. For teachers, understanding digital multilingualism is important for engaging students and for critically evaluating the linguistic landscape they inhabit.

### Multilingualism in education

Kenya's education system is inherently multilingual, yet the policy and practice of medium of instruction often privilege English and Kiswahili at the expense of mother tongues. The MTB-MLE policy (mother tongue in grades 1–3, transition to Kiswahili and English) aims to build on students' existing linguistic resources, but implementation is uneven. Teachers in multilingual classrooms face the challenge of managing students' diverse language backgrounds while maintaining curriculum goals. **Translanguaging** in the classroom — allowing students to use their full linguistic repertoires to discuss, analyse, and produce texts — can be a powerful pedagogical strategy that validates students' home languages while developing their English proficiency.

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

### Short answer

1. Define code-switching and distinguish it from translanguaging.
2. What is the matrix language frame model, and how does it explain code-switching?
3. Describe Sheng and explain its social function in Kenyan urban communities.
4. How does digital communication extend multilingual practices in Kenya? Give two examples.
5. What is the translanguaging framework, and who are its key proponents?
6. Explain why Sheng is considered a legitimate linguistic variety rather than 'broken' language.
7. How does multilingualism in the classroom relate to the MTB-MLE policy?

### Essay questions

8. 'Code-switching is a skilled communicative practice, not a sign of linguistic deficiency.' Discuss with reference to Kenyan contexts.
9. Compare code-switching and translanguaging as frameworks for understanding multilingualism. Which is more useful for analysing Kenyan classroom discourse?
10. How does Sheng reflect and shape youth identity in Kenya? Use linguistic examples to support your answer.
11. 'Digital communication has transformed multilingual practices in Kenya.' Evaluate this claim.

### Objective / passage-based

12. (Difficulty: Easy) Which concept describes drawing on a speaker's full linguistic repertoire as one integrated resource? (A) Code-switching (B) Translanguaging (C) Monolingualism (D) Language attrition
13. (Difficulty: Medium) Read the following Kenyan social media post and identify two instances of code-switching or translanguaging: 'Niko sokoni, nikamwambia mwanangu atupe kelele ya Kiswahili si English.'
14. (Difficulty: Hard) Given a classroom transcript where a teacher and students switch between English, Kiswahili, and Sheng, analyse the functions of each switch (e.g. clarification, solidarity, identity) and evaluate the pedagogical implications.
