## Unit overview

Teaching English and Literature in Secondary Schools I equips students with the principles and methods needed to teach English language, grammar and literature effectively in the Kenyan secondary school. The Unit covers lesson planning, resource use, group work, differentiation for mixed-ability classes, and assessment of the KCSE literature and English components. It bridges the gap between literary and linguistic knowledge and the practical demands of the classroom, preparing students to design lessons that integrate language and literature study in ways that are both pedagogically sound and contextually appropriate for Kenyan schools.

A central concern of the Unit is the relationship between language teaching and literature teaching. The Communicative Language Teaching (CLT) approach prioritises real communication — pair work, role-play, information gaps — and can be extended to literature through reader-response strategies and shared reading. For Kenyan classrooms, integrating Kiswahili and local languages supports comprehension and validates students' multilingual identities. The Unit also addresses the specific demands of the Kenya Certificate of Secondary Education (KCSE), which tests comprehension, grammar and usage, essay writing, functional writing and literature through a combination of passage-based and essay questions.

By the end of this Unit, students should be able to apply appropriate teaching approaches to English and Literature lessons, design lesson plans that integrate language and literature, and use formative and summative assessment strategies that reward both content and the clear organisation of ideas. The Unit provides the pedagogical foundation for the literary content covered in the other Year 2 Units.

## Learning outcomes

- Apply approaches such as the communicative approach, reader-response and shared reading to English and Literature lessons.
- Design lesson plans that integrate language and literature study for the Kenyan secondary classroom.
- Use formative and summative assessment strategies appropriate to English and Literature, including KCSE-style tasks.
- Integrate Kiswahili and mother-tongue resources to support English comprehension and engagement with literature.
- Differentiate instruction for mixed-ability classes using tiered tasks, choice boards and scaffolding.
- Analyse KCSE examination patterns and mark schemes to inform teaching and revision strategies.

## Detailed notes

### Communicative Language Teaching (CLT) in the literature classroom

Communicative Language Teaching (CLT) prioritises real communication over mechanical drill. In the literature classroom, CLT principles translate into activities that engage students with texts as living communication: pair discussions of character motivation, role-play of scenes, information-gap tasks in which students share different parts of a story and must negotiate meaning together. The language arts approach treats reading, writing and speaking as integrated, so that a lesson on a Shakespearean scene might combine close reading, oral performance and creative writing. For Kenyan teachers, CLT offers a student-centred alternative to the lecture-and-translation methods that have historically dominated, and it aligns with the competence-based curriculum reforms promoted by KICD.

### Shared reading and the unseen passage strategy

Shared reading involves the teacher and students reading a text together, with the teacher modelling fluent reading and guiding discussion of meaning, structure and language. This approach builds confidence, especially for students who struggle with independent reading, and creates a shared interpretive community in the classroom. The unseen passage strategy — in which students encounter a text they have not studied beforehand and must analyse it using the skills they have developed — is central to KCSE assessment. Teachers need to train students in the specific skills required: identifying the narrator's perspective, analysing language and structure, inferring meaning from context, and organising a coherent response. Regular practice with unseen passages, using a range of genres and periods, builds the transferable skills that KCSE demands.

### KCSE patterns and assessment design

The KCSE English examination has distinct components: comprehension, grammar and usage, essay writing, letter and article writing, and functional writing. The Literature component requires study of set books — typically a novel, a drama text and a collection of poetry or short stories — with questions that include passage-based analysis and broader essay responses. Formative assessment — quizzes, peer review, oral presentations, journal writing — should feed into and inform summative assessment, helping students identify gaps before the national examination. Mark schemes reward both content knowledge and the clear organisation of ideas, so teachers should train students in structured essay writing from the earliest stages. Understanding KCSE patterns is not about teaching to the test but about ensuring that students are equipped with the skills the examination measures.

### Integrating Kiswahili and mother-tongue in English teaching

Kenyan students enter secondary school with varying levels of English proficiency, often shaped by their home language and the quality of primary-level English instruction. Integrating Kiswahili and mother-tongue resources supports comprehension and validates students' linguistic identities. For example, discussing a literary text first in Kiswahili or in the students' mother-tongue before moving to English can deepen understanding and build confidence. Code-switching in classroom discussion should be treated as a resource rather than a problem. The 2010 Constitution recognises the role of Kiswahili and the diversity of Kenyan languages, and the Competency-Based Curriculum (CBC) reforms have introduced MTB-MLE at the primary level, creating a stronger foundation for English acquisition. Secondary teachers need to build on this foundation rather than treating students as though they arrive with a blank linguistic slate.

### Lesson planning for mixed-ability classes

Mixed-ability classes are the norm in Kenyan secondary schools, and differentiation is essential. Tiered tasks — the same topic addressed at different levels of complexity — allow all students to engage with the material at an appropriate level. Choice boards give students options for how they demonstrate their learning (written essay, oral presentation, creative response). Scaffolding — temporary support structures such as sentence starters, graphic organisers and model paragraphs — helps struggling students access the same content as their more able peers. Effective lesson planning for mixed-ability classes requires anticipating the range of needs in the room and designing activities that can be adapted in real time. Group work, when structured carefully, can be a powerful tool: pairing students of different abilities and assigning clear roles ensures that all members contribute and learn.

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

### Short answer (moderate)
1. What are the key principles of the Communicative Language Teaching (CLT) approach?
2. Explain how shared reading can support struggling readers in a Kenyan secondary classroom.
3. What are the main components of the KCSE English examination?
4. How can Kiswahili be integrated into an English Literature lesson without undermining English proficiency?
5. Define 'scaffolding' in the context of lesson planning and give one example.

### Essay questions (challenging)
1. 'The unseen passage is the most valuable component of the KCSE English paper.' Evaluate this claim with reference to teaching practice.
2. How can a teacher differentiate instruction in a mixed-ability English Literature class? Discuss with specific examples of activities and strategies.
3. Analyse the relationship between language teaching and literature teaching in the Kenyan secondary curriculum. How can they be integrated effectively?

### Objective / passage-based (moderate to challenging)
1. A teacher wants to use shared reading with a class of mixed-ability students reading *The River Between* by Ngugi wa Thiong'o. Which of the following is the best first step? (a) assign independent reading (b) read aloud while students follow the text (c) ask students to summarise before reading (d) give a vocabulary test.
2. In a KCSE literature essay, students are expected to (a) memorise the plot (b) analyse language, structure and meaning (c) translate the passage into Kiswahili (d) compare the text to a film adaptation.
3. Passage-based: Read the following excerpt and identify the narrative voice: 'We had walked for hours through the forest, and I could hear the birds long before I saw them.' (a) first-person (b) third-person omniscient (c) second-person (d) stream of consciousness.
4. Multiple choice: MTB-MLE stands for (a) Mother Tongue-Based Multilingual Education (b) Multilingual Teaching by Alternative Methods (c) Mother Tongue Bridge to Multilingual Education (d) Multilingual Teaching Based on Mother Languages.
