## Unit overview

Applied linguistics is the branch of linguistics that seeks to solve real language problems — in classrooms, courts, hospitals, media and the home. This Unit gives you the foundational concepts that English and Literature teachers must carry into the classroom: how words are built, how sentences are put together, how meaning is layered beyond the dictionary, how language works in conversation, and — crucially for Kenya — how first and second languages are acquired, how learners make errors, and how the multilingual learner can be supported. The approach is comparative and local: we link every concept to the Kenyan classroom, where learners move between an ethnic mother tongue, Kiswahili and English, often within a single sentence.

## Learning outcomes

- Identify and analyse the structure of words (morphemes, allomorphs, derivation and inflection).
- Describe basic syntactic constituents and processes in English.
- Distinguish lexical, conceptual and contextual components of meaning.
- Analyse how cohesion and turn-taking organise spoken and written discourse.
- Explain first and second language acquisition theories and their classroom implications.
- Diagnose learner errors and relate them to interlanguage development.
- Apply the principle of communicative competence to lesson and assessment design.
- Evaluate reading and writing instruction for multilingual Kenyan learners.

## Detailed notes

### Morphology — the architecture of words

A **morpheme** is the smallest meaning-bearing unit. *Unhappiness* splits into three morphemes: *un-* (negation), *happy* (free/root), *‑ness* (noun-forming suffix). **Allomorphs** are variant pronunciations of a morpheme in different environments — the English plural has /s/, /z/ and /ɪz/ allomorphs (*cats*, *dogs*, *boxes*). Two kinds of affix are central: **derivational** (creates new words; *happy → unhappy*, *teach → teacher*) and **inflectional** (does not change category; *teach → teaches*, *walk → walked*). Languages differ in how productive they are — English relies heavily on word order, while Kiswahili packs a single verb with subject, object, tense, mood and numeral markers. For teachers, recognising affixation helps explain why learners misspell (*accomodation* for *accommodation*) and why teaching morphology speeds vocabulary growth.

### Syntax — building sentences

Syntax studies how words combine into phrases and sentences. A **constituent** is a group of words that functions as a unit (e.g. a noun phrase *the tall student* or a verb phrase *is reading*). English is broadly **SVO** (subject–verb–object), but scrambling, clefting and topicalisation are possible. **Agreement** requires the verb to match its subject for number and person (*the student is*, *the students are*), a perennial source of learner error. **Subcategorisation** describes what complements a verb takes (*believe* takes that-clause or noun-phrase; *depend* takes *on*). **Movement** (e.g. *What did you see?*) and **binding** (reflexives like *himself*) reveal the underlying abstract structure. Tree-diagramming, taught in Kenyan HSK/linguistics courses, makes these relations visible.

### Semantics — meaning in language

Semantics studies meaning. **Lexical semantics** looks at word meanings and their relations: **synonymy** (big/large), **antonymy** (hot/cold), **hyponymy** (rose is a hyponym of flower), **meronymy** (hand is a meronym of body). **Sense vs. reference**: *the morning star* and *the evening star* have the same reference (Venus) but different senses. **Polysemy** (bank: riverbank, financial bank) and **homonymy** (bare/bear) challenge vocabulary teaching. Meaning also depends on context: **deixis** (I/you, today, here) shifts with speaker and time — essential for teaching English in Kenyan contexts where “today” and “here” travel between languages. Lexical semantics feeds directly into reading comprehension and vocabulary strategy.

### Discourse analysis — language beyond the sentence

Discourse studies language in use. **Cohesion** is the glue: **reference** (pronouns *he*, *they*; determiners *the*, *a*), **substitution/conjunction** (and, because), **elision**, **cross-referencing** (lexical repetition), and **collocation** (the words that habitually co-occur). **Coherence** is the mental work of the listener/reader: we infer connections the text leaves unsaid. **Turn-taking** governs conversation (Sacks, Schegloff & Jefferson): speaker holds the floor, then yields; overlaps and gaps are managed by rules. **Register** (field, tenor, mode) classifies texts — a classroom interaction has a different register than a newspaper article. In Kenyan multilingual talk, code-switching itself is a resource that creates new, mixed registers.

### Sociolinguistics and the Kenyan context

Sociolinguistics links language and society. Variation correlates with **social class**, **region**, **gender** and **age** (think Nairobi Sheng vs. rural Kĩsũ). Kenya has roughly 70 languages grouped into Niger-Congo, Nilo-Saharan, Cushitic and Nilotic families; English and Kiswahili are official. Most Kenyans are **multilingual**, and **code-switching** (English ↔ Kiswahili ↔ mother-tongue within an utterance) is norm, not error. **Diglossia** assigns functions: English/Highs for education, administration and media; mother-tongues/Lows for home and ritual. The 2010 Constitution and the **mother-tongue-based multilingual education (MTB-MLE)** policy aim to teach literacy in the first language in Grades 1–3 — with mixed results due to textbook shortages and teacher-proficiency gaps. **Language planning** decisions (which language teaches which subject, when) shape equity.

### Language acquisition — how languages are learned

First language acquisition (L1A) follows a universal sequence (babbling → one-word → two-word → complex syntax), with a hypothesised **critical period** for native-like phonology. **Krashen’s Input Hypothesis** holds that learners acquire language best through **comprehensible input** at “i+1” with a low **affective filter** (motivation, anxiety, self-confidence matter). **Swain’s Output Hypothesis** adds that producing language — especially when pushed to explain, correct or compare — drives development from a “compensatory” to “productive” stage. **Long’s Interaction Hypothesis** highlights negotiation of meaning (clarification requests, recasts) in conversation. **Cognitive approaches** model SLA like any skilled performance: noticing, practice, feedback. For Kenyan learners, age of English exposure is often later than in monolingual settings, so explicit attention to phonology and syntax alongside rich input is vital.

### Error analysis and interlanguage

Learner language is a rule-governed **interlanguage** that develops toward the target language. Errors are **transitional**, not failures: they reveal the learner’s current system and how it maps (or not) onto English. **Error analysis** collects samples, classifies each error by type (omission/addition/distortion), identifies **source** (overgeneralisation of English rules like *mouses*; L1 transfer from the mother tongue; communication strategies), and **remediation**. For example, the regular past-tense rule (-ed) is overgeneralised to *goed* because English normally marks past that way; Kĩsũ speakers may drop the copula (*She tall*) by L1 transfer. Teaching should exploit these errors as learning moments, not merely correct them.

### Communicative competence and teaching

**Communicative competence** (Hymes 1972) is the ability to use language appropriately and effectively for communicative purposes. Modern models include four strands — **grammatical**, **sociolinguistic**, **discourse** and **strategic** competence (Canale & ... ; widely cited framework). **Communicative Language Teaching (CLT)** operationalises this: real communication, integrated skills, focus on meaning first. In Kenya, this means tasks like role-plays in market English, storytelling circles that honour oral tradition, and using Kiswahili to check understanding. The CEFR’s can-do statements help sequence and assess what learners can actually do. **Assessment** should be balanced — reading, writing, listening, speaking — and aligned to outcomes, not just discrete-point grammar tests.

### Reading and writing development

Reading develops through **decoding → fluency → comprehension**. Before reading, activate prior knowledge and predict; during, question, summarise and clarify vocabulary; after, discuss themes and connect to experience. For **writing**, the process approach (pre-writing, drafting, peer feedback, revising, editing, publishing) works across narrative, descriptive, expository and argumentative genres. In multilingual Kenyan contexts, explicit paragraphing (topic sentence, evidence, analysis) and attention to grammar within meaningful tasks improve both accuracy and fluency. Literacy in a known first language strongly supports English literacy — a key argument for MTB-MLE.

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

### Short answer
1. **(Easy)** Define morpheme, allomorph and affix. Give one example of each.
2. **(Easy)** What is the difference between derivation and inflection? Give one English example each.
3. **(Medium)** Draw a simple constituent-tree (labeling NP, VP, etc.) for: *The boy kicked the ball.*
4. **(Medium)** Distinguish sense from reference. Illustrate with *the morning star* / *the evening star*.
5. **(Medium)** List three cohesive devices and give an example of each.
6. **(Hard)** Explain how Krashen’s affective filter interacts with motivation in a Kenyan secondary classroom.
7. **(Hard)** Classify the following learner error and identify its likely source: “He *goed* to school yesterday.”
8. **(Hard)** Describe the four strands of communicative competence and give a classroom implication of each.

### Essay questions
1. **(Medium)** “Error analysis reveals the learner’s interlanguage rather than the learner’s failure.” Discuss with reference to first and second language acquisition.
2. **(Hard)** Evaluate the value of the interaction hypothesis for teaching English to Kenyan learners who are also acquiring Kiswahili and a mother-tongue.

### Objective / passage-based
Read the following learner utterance, then answer: *“Mama, I go school yesterday and I see my friend but he no come.”*
1. **(Medium)** Identify TWO grammatical errors and name the English rule each violates.
2. **(Easy)** Which stage of Kĩsũ/English bilingual development does this best illustrate?
3. **(Hard)** Suggest ONE focused activity a teacher could use to address tense/aspect marking.
