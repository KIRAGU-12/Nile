## Unit overview

Introduction to Prose Fiction develops the close-reading skills introduced in ENG 111 by focusing specifically on narrative fiction in prose — short stories and novel extracts. The unit examines how narrative meaning is constructed through formal elements: plot organisation, characterisation, point of view, setting, narrative voice and **focalisation**. For Kenyan students, the unit offers a framework for reading both African and European prose traditions, with particular attention to Kenyan set texts such as Grace Ogot's "The Promised Gift" and Meja Mwangi's *Going Down River Road*. It also encourages students to connect narrative technique to social and cultural context — how a writer's choice of narrator shapes what the reader knows and feels.

By the end of the semester, students should be able to identify the structural elements of a narrative, compare different narrative techniques, and write analytical essays that argue how form shapes meaning. The unit prepares students for the longer essay requirements of the KCSE and university examination, where questions often require detailed commentary on character, setting or narrative perspective in a prose text.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this unit, students should be able to:
- Identify and analyse the major elements of narrative fiction: plot, character, setting, point of view, voice and focalisation.
- Compare different narrative techniques and points of view, including homodiegetic, heterodiegetic and free indirect discourse.
- Interpret how narrative form shapes meaning in specific short stories and novel extracts.
- Write analytical essays that support interpretive claims with close textual evidence.
- Relate narrative technique to themes such as colonialism, gender, urbanisation and cultural conflict in Kenyan fiction.
- Evaluate the relationship between oral and written narrative traditions in East African prose.

## Detailed notes

### Story, plot and narration

Narrative fiction tells a story through prose. The **story** is the chronological sequence of incidents; the **plot** is how those events are arranged and presented (the discourse). A plot usually moves through exposition → inciting incident → rising action → climax → falling action → resolution. The narrator decides what we see, when we see it and through whose eyes. **Narration** is the act of telling; **focalisation** is the perspective through which events are perceived — which may differ from the narrator's voice.

### Character and point of view

Characters may be **flat** (stereotypical, quickly recognised) or **round** (complex, surprising), **static** (unchanged) or **dynamic** (changed by events). **Point of view** is the narrator's position: first-person 'I', first-person multiple, third-person limited (one character's thoughts), third-person omniscient (all minds), or **free indirect discourse** (the narrator adopts a character's voice while remaining offstage). Free indirect discourse is crucial in the English novel because it allows the narrator to slip in and out of a character's consciousness, creating irony and psychological depth.

### Setting and atmosphere

Setting is not mere backdrop; it shapes character and theme. In Kenyan prose, setting often signals social hierarchy — the colonial bungalow, the rural homestead, the Nairobi slum — and the clash of cultures. Pay attention to sensory detail: smells, sounds, textures. These 'telling' details create mood, reveal class, or signal theme more powerfully than explicit statement.

### Kenyan prose traditions

East African prose fiction emerged from oral narrative traditions and was shaped by colonial education, missionary publishing and the postcolonial struggle for cultural autonomy. Grace Ogot's stories explore traditional Luo beliefs, Christian conversion and women's inner lives with quiet precision. Meja Mwangi's urban novels chart the gritty realities of postcolonial Kenyan society. Both writers use narrative technique — irony, focalisation, dialogue — to critique social injustice while maintaining literary artistry.

**Key terms:** story · plot · discourse · narration · focalisation · character (flat/round, static/dynamic) · point of view (first/second/third person; limited/omniscient) · free indirect discourse · setting · mood · voice · homodiegetic · heterodiegetic

## Practice exercises (exam revision)

**Short answer**
1. Define "focalisation" and explain how it differs from "point of view." (Medium)
2. Distinguish between a flat character and a round character, using a character from any short story studied in class. (Easy)
3. How does setting function as more than backdrop in one of the set texts? (Medium)

**Essay questions**
1. "Narrative form shapes meaning: the choice of narrator determines what the reader knows and feels." Discuss with reference to two short stories. (Hard)
2. Analyse how free indirect discourse is used in one of the set texts to create irony or psychological depth. (Medium)
3. Compare the treatment of urban space in two Kenyan short stories studied this semester. (Medium)

**Objective / passage-based**
1. Read the following extract from *Going Down River Road* and answer the questions that follow. (Hard)
   a. Identify the narrative point of view and comment on its reliability. (4 marks)
   b. How does Meja Mwangi use diction and imagery to establish social class? (4 marks)
   c. Comment on the effect of any one telling detail in the extract. (2 marks)
